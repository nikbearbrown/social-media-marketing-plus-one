# Research Pass — Chapter 10: Building Your Own Map

**Working title:** Building Your Own Map — Domain calibration, your Delegate/Guard List
**Reader:** Working social media managers and marketing professionals already using AI
**Thesis frame:** AI handles pattern; humans handle judgment. Chapter 10 is the chapter where the reader stops borrowing the author's Delegate/Guard List and builds their own — calibrated to industry, regulatory regime, org structure, and brand posture.
**Research scope:** Domain-specific calibration (B2B vs B2C; finance, healthcare, pharma; agency vs in-house; founder-led vs corporate). Workflow audit methods. Personal map-building.

## 1. Primary Sources

These are the canonical, citable, mostly evergreen sources to anchor the chapter. Practitioners can verify them.

**Regulatory primary sources (the documents that actually govern what can be delegated to AI in regulated industries)**

- **FINRA Regulatory Notice 17-18 (April 2017)** — "Guidance on Social Networking Websites and Business Communications" plus the original Regulatory Notice 10-06 and 11-39. Defines what counts as a "communication with the public" for broker-dealers, what requires principal pre-approval, what must be retained as a business record. The "static vs. interactive" content distinction is the load-bearing concept for any AI delegation map in financial services social. Available on finra.org.
- **SEC Marketing Rule (Rule 206(4)-1), adopted Dec 2020, compliance date Nov 4, 2022** — the modernized Investment Advisers Act marketing rule. Replaces the old Advertising Rule and Cash Solicitation Rule. Defines testimonials, endorsements, performance advertising, third-party ratings. SEC adopting release and FAQ on sec.gov. This is the doc that determines whether an AI-drafted LinkedIn post by a registered investment adviser is compliant.
- **FDA Guidance for Industry: "Internet/Social Media Platforms with Character Space Limitations" (June 2014, draft)** and **"Internet/Social Media Platforms: Correcting Independent Third-Party Misinformation About Prescription Drugs and Medical Devices" (June 2014, draft)** — still the only FDA-specific social media guidance for pharma. The fact that these are still drafts after 12 years is itself the story. Available on fda.gov.
- **HIPAA Privacy Rule, 45 CFR Part 164** — the actual regulation, plus HHS guidance "HIPAA & Social Media" (updated 2023). Defines Protected Health Information (PHI) and the eighteen identifiers. Any AI delegation map for healthcare marketing has to live downstream of this.
- **FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255 (revised 2023)** — covers influencer disclosures, fake reviews, "material connection" definitions, and the 2023 update on AI-generated reviews. The 2024 "Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials" (16 CFR Part 465) is also primary now.
- **EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689, in force Aug 1 2024, phased application through 2027)** — Article 50 transparency obligations for AI-generated content (including deepfakes and synthetic text "informing the public on matters of public interest"). Article 52 obligations for general-purpose AI. Relevant for any EU-facing brand's delegation map.
- **GDPR Article 22** — automated decision-making protections. Relevant when AI is doing audience segmentation or routing customer service.

**Platform-published primary sources (use sparingly — these age fastest)**

- Meta Business Help Center documentation on automated content rules and Advantage+ campaign automation.
- TikTok's Creative Codes and Branded Content Policy.
- LinkedIn's Professional Community Policies and Generative AI labeling policy (2024).
- X/Twitter Developer Agreement and Policy (post-2023 changes).
- *Treat all platform docs as illustrative, not canonical. They change quarterly.*

**Academic / framework primary sources**

- **Daugherty, Paul R. and H. James Wilson — "Human + Machine: Reimagining Work in the Age of AI" (Harvard Business Review Press, 2018; updated 2024 edition).** The "missing middle" framing (humans complementing AI, AI complementing humans) is the closest precedent for the AI+1 delegation logic. Cite carefully — their framing is more bullish than the AI+1 thesis.
- **Brynjolfsson, Erik — "The Turing Trap: The Promise & Peril of Human-Like Artificial Intelligence" (Daedalus, Spring 2022).** Argues that AI designed to substitute for humans produces worse economic outcomes than AI designed to augment. Direct theoretical underpinning for the Delegate/Guard split.
- **Autor, David — "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? The History and Future of Workplace Automation" (Journal of Economic Perspectives, Summer 2015).** The "Polanyi's Paradox" framing — we know more than we can tell — explains why tacit judgment work resists delegation. Foundational for Chapter 10's Guard List logic.
- **Fountaine, Tim, Brian McCarthy, and Tamim Saleh — "Building the AI-Powered Organization" (Harvard Business Review, July–Aug 2019).** The org-design piece. Useful for the agency vs. in-house calibration section.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review / BCG annual "Artificial Intelligence and Business Strategy" survey (2017–2024).** The 2023 report ("Achieving Individual—and Organizational—Value with AI") has direct data on workflow-level AI adoption gaps. Cite the most recent year available at writing.

**Workflow / process audit primary sources**

- **ISO 9001:2015 process approach** and the related Six Sigma DMAIC (Define-Measure-Analyze-Improve-Control) framework — these are the legacy, evergreen workflow audit methods. Useful as scaffolding: practitioners are doing a workflow audit, they just need a vocabulary.
- **Hammer, Michael — "Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate" (HBR, July–Aug 1990).** The original BPR essay. Hammer's argument — that automating a broken process just produces a faster broken process — is exactly the trap a naive AI delegation map falls into.
- **Womack, James and Daniel Jones — "Lean Thinking" (1996, updated 2003).** Value-stream mapping as a method. The Delegate/Guard map is essentially a value-stream map where the "machine" lane is AI and the "operator" lane is human judgment.

## 2. State of the Field (as of May 2026)

What practitioners are currently doing, arguing, and getting wrong about domain-calibrated AI delegation in social marketing.

**The dominant practitioner conversation is binary, not calibrated.** Most published material (LinkedIn thought leadership, marketing podcasts, agency white papers) treats AI in social as either (a) a productivity multiplier — "10x your output" — or (b) an existential threat to brand authenticity. Almost nothing in the trade press treats it as a delegation problem requiring calibration to industry conditions. Chapter 10 is filling a real gap.

**B2B vs B2C is the most-discussed split, and it's the wrong primary axis.** Trade press constantly contrasts B2B (long sales cycles, high-trust, LinkedIn-heavy) with B2C (high volume, low-trust, multi-platform). Useful but shallow. The more load-bearing splits are:
- **Regulated vs. unregulated.** A regulated B2C brand (consumer pharma, retail banking) has more in common with a regulated B2B brand (institutional asset management) than with an unregulated B2C brand.
- **Accountability locus.** Founder-led brands (Patagonia under Chouinard, Duolingo under von Ahn, Liquid Death under Cessario) have an irreducibly human voice because the accountability runs to a named person. Corporate brands diffuse accountability across a comms team. Different delegation maps.
- **Channel-dependent vs. channel-incidental.** A brand whose business depends on a single channel (a TikTok-native DTC brand) has a very different Guard List than one for whom social is a brand asset among many.

**Agency vs. in-house creates different failure modes.**
- Agencies face pressure to show AI-driven productivity gains to justify retainers. The failure mode is over-delegation: AI-generated content for fifteen clients that all sound the same.
- In-house teams face pressure to do more with the same headcount. The failure mode is under-delegation: still drafting every caption manually because "the brand voice is too specific" — which is sometimes true and sometimes a story the team tells itself.

**The "compliance review" bottleneck is the unsung gating function in regulated industries.** In finance, pharma, and healthcare, the rate-limiting step on social content is not creation — it's compliance review. AI can 10x the creation rate, but if compliance review takes 48 hours per post, the production-line gain is zero. Several pharma and financial services firms have started experimenting with AI-assisted *compliance review* (training models on past approval decisions). This is operationally significant and largely undiscussed in the marketing trade press.

**The "shadow AI" problem.** Surveys from Salesforce (2023, 2024) and Microsoft Work Trend Index (2024, 2025) show that the majority of knowledge workers using AI at work are using personal accounts on unapproved tools. In regulated social marketing this is a compliance disaster waiting to happen. Chapter 10 must address it.

**Generative AI labels are becoming load-bearing.** Meta's AI content labeling (May 2024 onward), TikTok's AIGC label (Sept 2023), LinkedIn's content credentials integration (2024), and EU AI Act Article 50 transparency obligations are all converging. The disclosure question is moving from "should we tell people?" to "what counts as enough disclosure?" Practitioners need a framework for this in their personal map.

**Open empirical question: does AI-generated content underperform once labeled?** Early platform data is contested. A 2024 University of Zurich study found AI-labeled content received fewer reactions on LinkedIn. Meta and TikTok have not released equivalent data. Treat as live and unresolved.

**Workflow audit methods in marketing are underdeveloped.** Operations teams have decades of process mapping methodology. Marketing teams generally have campaign briefs and editorial calendars but no equivalent of a value-stream map. Chapter 10 can borrow heavily from manufacturing and software engineering (value-stream mapping; Wardley maps for capability evolution; the "swim lane" diagram from BPR) and offer a marketing-specific synthesis.

## 3. Application Domain — Social Media Marketing

Specific scenarios, examples, and edge cases for the chapter.

**Industry-specific calibration examples** (each needs at least one concrete, recent scenario)

- **Pharma (Rx).** The hardest case. Every social post about a branded drug requires fair balance (risks disclosed alongside benefits), is subject to FDA submission requirements (Form 2253 for promotional materials), and lives downstream of an MLR (Medical, Legal, Regulatory) review process. The Delegate List is narrow: AI can draft unbranded disease-awareness content, help summarize MLR feedback, generate caption variants of an already-approved core claim. The Guard List is wide: any claim about efficacy, safety, off-label use, adverse event response. A useful concrete scenario: an oncology brand's response to a patient's public post about side effects. AI cannot make the call to engage or not.
- **Financial services (RIA / broker-dealer).** Static content (a "pinned" educational post about diversification) gets one compliance treatment; interactive content (a real-time response in comments) gets another. AI can draft static content for principal pre-approval; AI cannot author interactive responses without supervision. The FINRA archiving requirement means every AI-generated draft (used or not) may need to be retained — a workflow design problem Chapter 10 should name.
- **Healthcare (provider system).** PHI is the third rail. Any patient testimonial requires HIPAA authorization. AI can draft generic wellness content; AI cannot touch anything that comes through patient communications without a privacy-safe pipeline. The Cleveland Clinic, Mayo Clinic, and academic medical centers have published their own social media policies — useful as primary sources for in-chapter examples (cite specific clinic policies as documented examples, not endorsements).
- **Legal (Bar-regulated).** State bar advertising rules treat lawyer social posts as advertising. AI-drafted client testimonials are nearly always prohibited. AI can draft general legal education content; AI cannot characterize case outcomes or solicit specific clients. Variation by state — Florida, New York, California have stricter rules. Note ABA Model Rule 7.1–7.5 and the 2018 amendments.
- **Public companies (SEC Reg FD).** Regulation Fair Disclosure (2000) means material non-public information cannot be selectively disclosed via social. SEC has affirmed Reg FD applies to social media posts (2013 guidance, Netflix case). AI cannot make the call about what counts as material.
- **Consumer packaged goods (FTC-only).** The lightest regulatory regime. The delegation map widens significantly. Failure modes here are brand-voice and influencer-disclosure, not regulatory.
- **B2B SaaS.** Few regulatory constraints; the constraint is sales-marketing alignment and account-based marketing context. AI can draft top-of-funnel content; the judgment call is about content that touches named accounts in active deal cycles. Salesperson context becomes the Guard List item.
- **DTC ecommerce.** High volume, high variant testing, paid-heavy. The Delegate List is wide; the Guard List is narrow but load-bearing — claims substantiation (FTC), influencer disclosure, return/shipping promises that become contractual.
- **Nonprofit / advocacy.** Charitable solicitation registration in many states (URS — Unified Registration Statement). Political speech has its own First Amendment landscape. AI cannot write fundraising appeals that misrepresent program impact (Form 990 lives downstream).

**Org structure calibration**

- **Agency.** The map has to work across multiple client industries. Suggest a brand-specific "delegation profile" document the agency builds with each client at onboarding, refreshed quarterly. The profile names: what AI tools are sanctioned, what content types AI may draft, what review steps are non-negotiable, what platform-specific Guards apply.
- **In-house team at large brand.** Coordination cost dominates. The map needs to integrate with the existing approval workflow (legal review, brand review, exec sign-off on certain topics). Suggest treating the map as a swim-lane diagram with AI as one lane.
- **Founder-led / personal brand.** The accountability locus is a single person. The Guard List is dominated by voice and stance. The Delegate List can be wider on production tasks (cross-posting, scheduling, analytics summaries) but narrower on anything attributed to the founder's voice. The founder's *judgment about which posts even get made* is the irreducibly human input.
- **Small business / sole proprietor.** Time-constrained, single-decision-maker. The map looks like a personal productivity system. Risk of over-delegation because the cost of any individual post is low. The Guard List should focus on the few topics that, if mishandled, would damage reputation in a small market.

**Workflow audit method — a chapter-specific protocol the reader can run**

Adapt value-stream mapping to social marketing. The reader, in roughly half a working day, can produce a calibrated map by:

1. **Inventory** every social marketing task they perform in a typical two-week cycle. Use the chapter list (Ch 3–9) as a starter taxonomy; add brand-specific tasks.
2. **Classify each task** along three axes: pattern-shape vs. judgment-shape (Ch 2 framework); regulatory weight (none / light / heavy); reputational tail-risk (low / medium / high). The three axes produce a 3D placement that suggests a delegation posture.
3. **Audit the tooling**: what AI tool, if any, currently touches this task? Personal account or enterprise? Logged or unlogged? Reviewed or unreviewed?
4. **Draft the Delegate List** — tasks where AI is doing the bulk of the work and a human reviews.
5. **Draft the Guard List** — tasks where humans do the bulk of the work and AI assists at most (research, summary, draft outline).
6. **Identify the boundary cases** — tasks that should move from one list to the other given current AI capability and current org constraints. These get re-audited next quarter.
7. **Test the map** — run a one-week trial against the map; document where it broke; revise.

This is the chapter's centerpiece exercise. It produces a tangible artifact (the reader's personal map) and operationalizes the framework.

## 4. Thesis Connection — AI handles pattern; humans handle judgment

How Chapter 10's content reinforces and operationalizes the core thesis.

**The map is the thesis made operational at the level of one person's job.** Chapters 1–2 state the boundary; Chapters 3–9 walk it task by task using the author's calibration; Chapter 10 forces the reader to redo the calibration for their conditions. Domain calibration is not a footnote — it is the proof that the framework is not a recipe. A recipe would produce the same Delegate/Guard List for everyone; the thesis predicts the lists will vary by domain because the *judgment surface* varies by domain.

**Specifically:**
- A regulated industry has more legally consequential judgment per post than an unregulated one. The Guard List necessarily widens.
- A founder-led brand has more voice-defining judgment per post. The Guard List shifts toward voice and stance, away from compliance.
- An agency serving multiple industries has a *meta*-judgment problem: choosing which delegation profile applies to which client. That meta-judgment is irreducibly human and is itself an item on the agency's Guard List.

**The map exercise also reinforces the thesis by surfacing the reader's tacit judgment.** When practitioners try to articulate why they personally draft a certain kind of post rather than delegating it, they often discover the answer is "I can't quite say." That is Polanyi's Paradox showing up in their own workflow — direct evidence that some of what they do is judgment-shaped, not pattern-shaped. The chapter should encourage this as a productive discovery, not a failure of articulation.

**The +1 in Chapter 10 is the practitioner themselves becoming a calibrator.** Earlier chapters position the human as the judge of specific outputs. Chapter 10 positions the human as the designer of their own delegation system. This is a step up in agency — and it is also the step where the reader stops being a consumer of the book and becomes a practitioner of the framework.

**A risk to manage in this chapter:** if domain calibration is presented as a checklist, it becomes a recipe by another name. The chapter should be explicit that the calibration *method* generalizes but the specific Delegate/Guard List is necessarily local. Re-state the thesis at the end: pattern is universal; judgment is local. The map is local because judgment is local.

## 5. Wayback Candidates — Historical Figures

Two to three lesser-known historical figures whose work models the chapter's argument. Diverse where possible.

**Candidate A — Lillian Moller Gilbreth (1878–1972), industrial engineer and psychologist.**
Co-founder, with husband Frank Gilbreth, of motion study and the discipline that became industrial engineering. Holder of a Ph.D. in applied psychology (Brown, 1915 — initially refused at Berkeley because she had not been in residence the requisite year). After Frank's sudden death in 1924 she ran the consultancy and raised twelve children. *Why she fits Chapter 10:* Gilbreth invented systematic workflow mapping — the "therblig" was their unit of irreducible motion, and the process chart their tool for seeing where work could be reorganized vs. where skill was irreducibly tacit. She also insisted on the human factor in process redesign in a way Taylor did not — Gilbreth's contribution was to make the worker's experience part of the system being measured. The Delegate/Guard map descends directly from this lineage. The lesser-known angle: most readers know Frank from *Cheaper by the Dozen*; few know that Lillian carried the intellectual program for forty years after his death and applied it to kitchen design (the L-shaped work triangle is hers) and to ergonomics for disabled veterans.

**Candidate B — W. Edwards Deming (1900–1993), statistician — used here as the under-read voice on *systems*, not the famous Toyota-quality story.**
Deming's well-known contribution is statistical process control and the Japanese quality movement. The under-read contribution, and the one that fits Chapter 10, is his insistence that 94% of problems in a system are system problems, not individual problems — meaning that telling a worker to "use AI better" without redesigning the system around them produces predictable failure. His "System of Profound Knowledge" (the 1990s synthesis published in *The New Economics*, 1993) explicitly separates the parts of a system that can be standardized from the parts that require local knowledge. That is structurally identical to the Delegate/Guard distinction. *Why he fits:* the chapter is teaching workflow redesign, and Deming is the canonical voice on workflow redesign as a humane practice rather than a mechanization push.

**Candidate C — Mary Parker Follett (1868–1933), management theorist.**
Follett is the candidate to lean on for the chapter's *judgment-is-local* point. Working in Boston in the early twentieth century, she developed a theory of management in which authority comes from "the law of the situation" — the right answer is determined by the specific facts of the case, and the manager's job is to surface those facts, not to impose a generic rule. Drucker called her the "prophet of management" and noted that the field forgot her for fifty years because her ideas didn't fit the Taylorist moment. *Why she fits Chapter 10:* the chapter's core argument is that the Delegate/Guard List is necessarily calibrated to local situation. Follett gave us the language for that nearly a century ago. She is also a strong representation choice — a woman doing serious management theory in the 1920s, whose erasure and rediscovery is itself a useful story about whose judgment gets taken seriously.

**(Alternate, if a fourth is wanted:) Taiichi Ohno (1912–1990).** Father of the Toyota Production System. Insisted that automation without "autonomation" (jidoka — machines that know when to stop and call a human) produces worse outcomes than no automation at all. Direct precedent for the AI+1 stance.

## 6. Pedagogy — How to Teach This

The chapter has to move the reader from reading about the framework to using it. Pedagogical design notes.

**The chapter is a workshop, not a lecture.** The reader does an exercise — building their own map — inside the chapter. The prose should be structured around the exercise, not around an exposition that lands at an exercise. Open with the audit prompt; let the reader's own answers carry the chapter.

**Use a single sustained worked example, plus contrast cases.** Pick one industry (suggestion: a mid-size RIA — concrete, regulated, common, not over-covered in the trade press) and walk the full audit from inventory to revised map. Then offer three short contrast cases (a founder-led DTC brand; a hospital system; a B2B SaaS startup) showing how the same method produces different maps. This makes "the method generalizes; the map doesn't" concrete.

**Build the Delegate List and Guard List as *living* documents.** Earlier chapters present them as fixed per task. Chapter 10 should introduce the idea of a quarterly review — what moved from Guard to Delegate, what moved back, why. This addresses the Chapter 11 question (capability trajectory) by giving the reader a maintenance protocol now.

**Make the workflow audit run inside a single working day.** The reader is busy. If the exercise takes a week to complete they will not do it. Time-box the audit (suggest 3–4 hours), structure it as a worksheet, and label optional deepening passes. Worksheet artifact lives at chapter end; copy-paste prompt block invokes Claude Code to help draft the map.

**Address the "I don't have authority to do this" objection directly.** Many readers — especially in mid-sized in-house teams and at agencies — cannot unilaterally adopt a delegation map. The chapter needs a section on *socializing* the map: how to present it to compliance, to legal, to a manager. Frame the map as a risk management document, not just a productivity document, because that is the framing that gets it adopted in regulated organizations.

**Cognitive load:** the chapter has more concepts in flight than most others (industry variation; org variation; workflow audit method; map exercise; review protocol). Use the chapter's anatomy from TIKTOC ruthlessly — Delegate List, Guard List, Do This, Never Do This — to keep the structural pattern recognizable. Resist the urge to introduce new terminology beyond "delegation profile" and "judgment surface."

**The exercise needs a worked output the reader can see before they try.** Include one fully filled-in Delegate/Guard map for the RIA worked example. Then a blank template. Then prompts for the contrast cases. This is the "I, We, You" structure from instructional design — model it, do it together, do it alone.

**End-of-chapter outputs the reader produces:**
1. A personal Delegate/Guard map (their own job).
2. A delegation profile for their primary brand or client.
3. A list of three to five boundary cases for quarterly review.
4. A drafted memo for socializing the map within their organization.

## 8. Open Questions

Things the chapter has to resolve before drafting, or has to flag and leave open.

1. **How many industries get full worked examples vs. brief mentions?** Recommend: one fully worked (RIA), three briefly worked (DTC, hospital, B2B SaaS), the others gestured to with one-sentence calibration notes. Otherwise the chapter bloats.
2. **Does the chapter take a position on whether AI-generated content should be disclosed beyond what platforms / regulators require?** This is a stance question. Recommend: yes — argue for disclosure as part of the Guard List for trust-dependent industries, name it as optional but recommended for others. Defending the stance is a footnote-sized argument.
3. **How prescriptive is the workflow audit method?** A protocol the reader follows step-by-step, or a set of principles they adapt? Recommend: protocol with explicit "adapt to your context" flags. Practitioners under time pressure follow protocols; principles read as homework.
4. **Does the chapter address AI-augmented compliance review explicitly?** This is a real and growing practice in finance and pharma. It deserves a paragraph, possibly a sidebar. Risk: it ages fast.
5. **Where does the agency/in-house split sit relative to the industry split?** Recommend: industry calibration is primary; org structure is a layer applied on top. Otherwise the chapter has a 2-axis classification problem and gets harder to read.
6. **How do we handle the "shadow AI" problem without scolding the reader?** The reader is likely using personal-account ChatGPT for work-stuff they should not be. The chapter should give them a clean path to surface it (audit step 3) without making it the centerpiece.
7. **Quarterly review protocol — how much detail?** Recommend: a one-page protocol at chapter end. Otherwise it sounds like good intentions.
8. **Should the chapter recommend specific enterprise AI products?** No. Product recommendations age in 6 months. Name categories (enterprise LLM seat with admin controls; logged AI workflow tools; compliance-trained models) and let the reader pick.

## 9. Sourcing Notes

Documentation hygiene for the chapter draft.

**Aging risk by source class (high to low):**
- Specific platform features and policies (Meta/TikTok/LinkedIn AI labeling, X policy specifics) — high risk. Cite with date, treat as illustrative.
- Specific AI products and capabilities (named ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini model versions and their context windows) — high risk. Prefer category-level language; cite specific models only in worked examples with date stamps.
- Industry adoption statistics (Salesforce State of Marketing AI, HubSpot State of Marketing, MIT Sloan / BCG AI surveys) — medium risk. Use the most recent year at writing; flag the year in the prose; do not anchor an argument to a single year's number.
- Regulatory documents (FINRA, SEC, FDA, HIPAA, FTC, EU AI Act) — lower risk for foundational regs; medium for guidance documents and updates. Always cite the citation (Notice / Rule / CFR / Article number) so a reader can verify the current version.
- Academic and HBR pieces from 2015–2020 (Brynjolfsson, Autor, Daugherty & Wilson, Fountaine et al.) — low risk. The framing has aged well.
- Historical figures (Gilbreth, Follett, Deming, Hammer) — very low risk. Use standard biographical sources (Drucker on Follett; Boettinger on Gilbreth; *Out of the Crisis* and *The New Economics* on Deming).

**Citation conventions:**
- Regulatory docs: cite by official number (e.g., "FINRA Regulatory Notice 17-18"), include adoption / effective date, link to .gov source where reasonable.
- Academic: standard format with year. Prefer DOI or stable URL.
- Industry reports: name the publisher, report title, year. Do not cite "according to a recent study" — specific or omit.
- Platform docs: include date accessed; flag as subject to change.

**Verification log to maintain during drafting:**
- Are all named brands currently operating as described? (Verify monthly during drafting.)
- Are all cited regulatory provisions current? (Spot-check via the issuing agency's site.)
- Are all named founders / executives still in role? (LinkedIn check during drafting.)
- Are all platform features still as described? (Spot-check on the platform within 30 days of draft completion.)

**Things to deliberately *not* source:**
- Predictions about which AI tools will dominate by 2027. Chapter 11 handles forecasting; Chapter 10 stays present-tense.
- Specific dollar figures on agency AI productivity gains. The literature is too noisy and the framing is too easily anchored to vendor self-report.
- Anecdotes about a single brand's AI win or loss without primary documentation (linked post-mortem, regulatory filing, public CEO statement). The trade press is full of recycled anecdotes; verify or omit.

**A note on the chapter's own aging:** Chapter 10 will age more slowly than Ch 3–9 because the *method* is more durable than the *task-specific delegation lists* of those chapters. Specific industry calibrations may age (e.g., new FDA pharma social guidance, EU AI Act phased compliance dates) but the audit protocol should survive. Design the chapter so the protocol is the load-bearing element and industry sections are updatable inserts.
