# Research Pass — Chapter 12: The +1 Practice

**Working title:** The +1 Practice — Integration, workflow, daily discipline
**Reader:** Working social media managers and marketing professionals already using AI
**Thesis frame:** AI handles pattern; humans handle judgment. Chapter 12 turns the framework into a practice — a set of habits, rituals, tools, and protocols that sustain the Delegate/Guard discipline daily and weekly across years, not just for the duration of a project.
**Research scope:** Habit formation and behavior design; professional practice maintenance under technological pressure; workflow templates and tooling; the discipline of integration; closing-the-book pedagogy.

## 1. Primary Sources

**Behavior design and habit formation — primary sources**

- **Fogg, BJ — "A Behavior Model for Persuasive Design" (Persuasive '09 proceedings, 2009)** and **"Tiny Habits: The Small Changes That Change Everything" (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2019).** Fogg Behavior Model (B = MAP — Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt). Tiny Habits' three-step formula (anchor moment / tiny behavior / celebration) is directly applicable to building a daily AI+1 discipline.
- **Wood, Wendy — "Good Habits, Bad Habits: The Science of Making Positive Changes That Stick" (FSG, 2019)**, drawing on her empirical work at USC (decades of papers on context-cued repetition). The 43% finding — that roughly 43% of daily behavior is habitual rather than deliberate — is foundational. Wood's empirical record on habit formation timelines is more rigorous than Lally et al. (2009) but both are citable.
- **Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts, Wardle — "How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world" (European Journal of Social Psychology, 2009).** The widely-cited (and widely-misquoted) "66 days on average, with wide variance" finding. The variance is the load-bearing point; the 66-day median is not.
- **Clear, James — "Atomic Habits" (Avery, 2018).** Popular synthesis. Useful for vocabulary (habit stacking, environment design, identity-based habits) but cite as synthesis not as primary research. The four-laws structure (cue / craving / response / reward) descends from Charles Duhigg's *The Power of Habit* (2012), which descends from cue-routine-reward loops in addiction research.
- **Duhigg, Charles — "The Power of Habit" (Random House, 2012).** Foundational popular treatment of the habit loop. The "keystone habit" idea is useful for Chapter 12's argument that the AI+1 audit is a keystone for sustained discipline.
- **Bandura, Albert — "Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control" (Freeman, 1997).** Classical source on self-efficacy as a determinant of sustained behavior. Relevant because the reader's belief that they can maintain the practice is itself a determinant of whether they will.

**Professional practice and skill maintenance — primary sources**

- **Ericsson, K. Anders, Krampe, Tesch-Römer — "The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance" (Psychological Review, 1993).** The deliberate practice paper. Cite alongside the corrective literature: **Macnamara, Hambrick, Oswald — "Deliberate Practice and Performance in Music, Games, Sports, Education, and Professions: A Meta-Analysis" (Psychological Science, 2014)**, which found deliberate practice explains less performance variance than the original paper claimed. The current synthesis: deliberate practice is necessary but not sufficient; context, talent, and structure matter. Useful for framing the AI+1 practice as deliberate but not magic.
- **Schön, Donald A. — "The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action" (Basic Books, 1983)** and **"Educating the Reflective Practitioner" (Jossey-Bass, 1987).** The two foundational books on reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action. The practice of reviewing one's own delegation decisions is exactly Schön's reflection-on-action.
- **Kolb, David A. — "Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development" (Prentice-Hall, 1984).** Kolb's cycle (concrete experience → reflective observation → abstract conceptualization → active experimentation) maps onto a weekly AI+1 review ritual.
- **Drucker, Peter F. — "Managing Oneself" (HBR, Jan 1999, originally Jan-Feb 1999).** Drucker's late essay on self-management as the durable knowledge-worker skill. The chapter's argument that the practitioner's own discipline is the load-bearing element is structurally Druckerian. *The Effective Executive* (1967) is the long form.
- **Newport, Cal — "Deep Work" (Grand Central, 2016)** and **"A World Without Email" (Portfolio, 2021).** Newport on attention as the contested resource of knowledge work. Direct relevance: the AI+1 practitioner needs protected time for the judgment layer, even as pattern-layer tasks compress.

**Implementation intentions and ritual design — primary sources**

- **Gollwitzer, Peter M. — "Implementation Intentions: Strong Effects of Simple Plans" (American Psychologist, 1999)** and follow-up meta-analyses (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 2006). The if-then planning literature. Effect sizes are moderate to large; the methodology is robust. Direct application: the AI+1 practice survives only if the practitioner has if-then plans for the recurring moments where the delegation call must be made.
- **Heath, Chip and Dan Heath — "Switch: How to Change Things When Change Is Hard" (Crown, 2010).** The "direct the rider / motivate the elephant / shape the path" framing. Useful as plain-English distillation for the chapter's tone.
- **Norcross, John C. et al. — Psychology of Behavior Change literature on New Year's resolutions and behavior maintenance.** Norcross's empirical work on what makes resolutions stick (commitment public, behavior specified, support social, monitoring active) maps directly onto sustaining the AI+1 practice past initial enthusiasm.

**Workflow and tooling — primary sources**

- **Allen, David — "Getting Things Done" (Penguin, 2001, revised 2015).** The two-minute rule, capture-clarify-organize-reflect-engage cycle. The chapter is not endorsing GTD wholesale, but the *weekly review* discipline is exactly the rhythm the AI+1 practice requires.
- **Forte, Tiago — "Building a Second Brain" (Atria, 2022).** CODE method (Capture, Organize, Distill, Express). Useful for the chapter's "delegation log" — the practitioner keeps a running record of what was delegated, what was guarded, and what the call surfaced.
- **Csíkszentmihályi, Mihály — "Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience" (Harper & Row, 1990).** Flow conditions (clear goals, immediate feedback, balance of challenge and skill). Useful as background: the AI+1 practice should produce more flow time for judgment work by removing pattern-work friction, not less.
- **Toyota Production System / kata literature.** Mike Rother's *Toyota Kata* (2009) on improvement kata and coaching kata is the most actionable workflow-as-practice source. The kata structure — small, repeated, deliberately structured practice toward an aspirational target — is exactly the spine for a sustained AI+1 discipline.
- **Anthropic, OpenAI, and major LLM vendor enterprise documentation on workflow integration patterns.** Cite specific docs with date; treat as illustrative.

**Failure literature — what goes wrong with new practices in busy professional lives**

- **Prochaska, DiClemente — Transtheoretical Model of behavior change (1983 onward), specifically the maintenance and termination stages.** The model's empirical claim that maintenance failure is its own stage with its own intervention requirements is directly relevant.
- **Mischel, Walter — "The Marshmallow Test: Why Self-Control Is the Engine of Success" (Little, Brown, 2014)**, with the corrective work from Watts, Duncan, Quan (Psychological Science, 2018) on the limits of the original studies. The current synthesis: self-control matters but context and resources matter more. Important for the chapter's tone — don't moralize about discipline; design the environment.
- **Baumeister and Tierney — "Willpower: Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength" (Penguin, 2011).** The ego depletion literature has been substantially undermined by failed replications (the 2016 Hagger et al. multi-lab study). Cite carefully — use to inform the chapter's stance that the practice should not depend on willpower.

## 2. State of the Field (as of May 2026)

**The trade-press conversation on "AI productivity" is almost entirely about acquisition, not maintenance.** A practitioner can find hundreds of articles on "how to start using AI in your marketing workflow" and almost none on "how to sustain a calibrated AI practice over three years as capability changes, tools change, and your role changes." Chapter 12 fills a real and conspicuous gap.

**Adoption-then-abandonment is the empirically dominant pattern.** Salesforce, Microsoft, and McKinsey workforce surveys 2023–2025 show a consistent pattern: high initial AI tool adoption, followed by stabilization at a smaller core use case, followed by abandonment when the tool changes or the user's role changes. The practitioners who sustain the practice are the ones who have a meta-practice — a way of integrating tools that is independent of the specific tool. This is what Chapter 12 is teaching.

**"Shadow practice" is the unwritten reality.** Even practitioners who have adopted enterprise AI tools often run a parallel personal practice (personal ChatGPT, personal Claude, a personal prompt library, a personal note system) that is more sophisticated than the sanctioned workflow. The chapter should acknowledge this without endorsing it as a long-term strategy; the meta-practice should be portable between personal and enterprise contexts.

**Three current schools of "AI workflow practice" in the trade conversation, none of which Chapter 12 should endorse wholesale:**

- **Tool-stack maximalism.** "Here are the 47 AI tools you should be using." Burnout-inducing, agnostic about judgment, ages in months.
- **One-tool-rules-them-all evangelism.** "Just use [Claude / ChatGPT / Gemini / Perplexity / etc.] for everything." Ignores the task-fit question Chapter 6 and Chapter 9 already established.
- **AI-skeptic restraint.** "Use AI minimally; rely on craft." Refuses the delegation question rather than answering it.

The AI+1 practice is none of these. It is a *discipline of judgment about delegation*, instrumented by whatever tools currently serve the discipline, sustainable because the discipline transfers between tools.

**The "AI literacy" movement is the closest professional analogue.** Industry bodies (IAB, ANA, AMA in marketing; broader work from MIT, Stanford, and OECD) are developing AI literacy frameworks. They tend to focus on *capability* literacy (what AI can do) over *discipline* literacy (how to integrate AI into a sustained professional practice). Chapter 12 is taking a position: discipline is the gap.

**The "operating rhythm" framing is gaining ground in marketing ops.** Marketing-ops practitioners increasingly talk about weekly cadences, monthly reviews, quarterly recalibrations as the necessary structure for sustained AI integration. This is consonant with the chapter's argument and worth citing where specific operating rhythm models are documented (e.g., HubSpot, Salesforce, and Drift have published variations).

**A practitioner stat worth tracking:** in 2024–2025 surveys (HubSpot State of Marketing AI; Marketing AI Institute reports), the gap between "tools available" and "tools sustainably integrated into workflow" has widened. Practitioners report using more AI tools than ever and feeling less productive than they expected. This is the symptom of an absent meta-practice; Chapter 12 prescribes the meta-practice.

**The closing-the-book pedagogy question.** Chapter 12 is the final chapter; it has to do work the rest of the book did not: it has to send the reader forward as a *practitioner* not a *reader*. The instructional-design literature on transfer (e.g., Perkins and Salomon, "Transfer of Learning," 1992; Barnett and Ceci, "When and Where Do We Apply What We Learn?" Psychological Bulletin, 2002) is unambiguous that transfer is much harder than acquisition. The chapter has to design for transfer explicitly: rituals, artifacts, prompts, community.

## 3. Application Domain — Social Media Marketing

What the daily and weekly AI+1 practice looks like in the working life of a social media manager or marketing professional.

**The daily practice — what the practitioner does in roughly 15–30 minutes a day to maintain the discipline.**

- **Morning anchor (5 min).** Look at today's Delegate/Guard map. Note which task families will be exercised today. Note any boundary cases that have surfaced in the last 24 hours.
- **Per-task delegation check (in-flow, < 30 sec per task).** Before starting a social marketing task, classify it: Delegate / Guard / boundary. Apply the chapter-relevant Do This / Never Do This. This is the if-then plan that prevents drift.
- **End-of-day delegation log (5 min).** Note the three to five most consequential delegation calls of the day. What was delegated, what was guarded, what surprised you, what you'd do differently. The log itself is small; the discipline of writing it is large.
- **Friction journal (optional, 5 min).** Note any place where the practice was hard today — where you over-delegated, under-delegated, or were uncertain. This feeds the weekly review.

**The weekly practice — roughly 30–60 minutes once a week.**

- **Weekly review (30 min).** Scan the delegation log. Identify: patterns of over-delegation; patterns of under-delegation; boundary cases that need a stance; tool failures that need a workaround. Update the personal Delegate/Guard map if a pattern is clear.
- **Brand voice audit (15 min).** Sample 10 pieces of content the brand produced this week (AI-touched or not). Read them as the audience. Note voice drift if any. This is the human-only audit of the AI-touched output.
- **Tool calibration (15 min, weekly or biweekly).** Note any change in tool capability — a model upgrade, a new feature, a new failure mode. Update prompt templates if needed.

**The monthly practice — roughly 90 minutes once a month.**

- **Map revision.** Move items between Delegate and Guard if the data supports it. Document the move.
- **Calibration check.** Pick five outputs from the past month. Ask: did the AI+1 practice produce a better output than no-AI would have, than full-AI would have? Honestly. The honest answer is the calibration.
- **Capability tracking.** Note one or two capability developments worth watching (this feeds the Chapter 11 forecasting register).

**The quarterly practice — roughly half a day.**

- **Full audit.** Re-run the Chapter 10 audit protocol. Has the map drifted? Has the org context changed? Have new task families emerged? Have regulatory conditions changed?
- **Practice retrospective.** Three honest questions: where did the practice serve the work? Where did the practice serve the brand? Where did the practice feel performative?
- **Forward calibration.** What is the next quarter's experiment?

**Tools that sustain the practice (categories, not products — products age in months).**

- **A delegation log.** Could be a markdown file, a Notion page, an Obsidian vault, a paper notebook. Format matters less than the discipline of writing it.
- **A prompt library tied to the map.** Each Delegate List task has at least one tested prompt. Each prompt has provenance (when written, when last tested, on which model).
- **A "guard rail" tag system.** Some way of marking content that touched AI, even if the disclosure does not go to the audience, so the practitioner has a private record.
- **A calendar ritual.** The daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly rhythms are calendared. Otherwise they don't happen.
- **A pair or small group.** The practice is more durable when one other practitioner is doing it alongside. Could be a colleague, a peer in another company, a Humanitarians AI workshop cohort.

**Specific failure modes the chapter should name and address.**

- **Tool drift.** A new model version subtly changes output quality. The practitioner doesn't notice because the practice doesn't include capability checks.
- **Voice drift.** AI-touched content slowly converges on a flattened brand voice. The practitioner doesn't notice because they're inside the workflow. The weekly voice audit catches this.
- **Discipline erosion under pressure.** Under quarterly deadlines, the practitioner over-delegates and skips the guard list. The chapter should acknowledge this is the most common failure mode and design for it (a 30-second delegation check is more robust under pressure than a 30-minute one).
- **Role drift.** The practitioner is promoted, changes companies, changes industries. The map has to move with them. The discipline of the practice transfers; the specific lists do not.
- **Capability drift.** Twelve months from now, several Guard List items may have moved to Delegate. The quarterly audit catches this.
- **Disengagement.** The practitioner finds the practice tedious and abandons it. The chapter should make peace with the fact that not every reader will sustain the full practice — and offer a minimum viable version (daily 5-minute anchor + monthly 15-minute audit) that captures most of the discipline at minimum cost.

**Integration with existing workflows.**

The chapter should explicitly position the AI+1 practice as integrating with existing workflows, not replacing them. The reader likely already has: a content calendar, a campaign brief format, a reporting cadence, a one-on-one with a manager. The AI+1 practice attaches to existing rituals where possible (delegation log lives alongside the content calendar; weekly review attaches to the existing weekly recap meeting). Where the practice requires its own ritual, name the time cost honestly.

**The practitioner's career arc and the practice.**

Three years from now, the practitioner who sustained the practice has a different kind of artifact: a longitudinal record of how their own delegation map changed as capability changed. This artifact is professionally valuable — it is evidence of judgment, calibration, and adaptation. Chapter 12 can name this explicitly as part of the long return on the practice.

## 4. Thesis Connection — AI handles pattern; humans handle judgment

**Chapter 12 closes the loop by making the thesis a *practice*, not a belief.** A reader can finish Chapters 1–11 holding the thesis as an idea — pattern is for AI, judgment is for humans — without ever having lived it. Chapter 12 insists that the thesis is only operative if it shows up in the practitioner's daily and weekly rhythm. Belief without practice is decorative; practice without belief is unsustainable; both together are the +1.

**Specifically, the practice operationalizes each axis of the thesis:**

- **Pattern → AI.** The practice includes specific rituals for using AI on pattern-shaped tasks: the prompt library tied to the Delegate List, the per-task delegation check, the calibration of AI output against brand baselines. This makes the "AI handles pattern" half of the thesis a daily action, not a daily attitude.
- **Judgment → human.** The practice includes specific rituals for protecting and developing judgment: the brand voice audit, the boundary case journal, the quarterly retrospective. These rituals ensure that the human layer is exercised, not atrophied. The chapter should be explicit: if you delegate the pattern work without exercising the judgment work, you do not become a better practitioner — you become a less practiced one.
- **+1 → the practitioner's accountable choice.** The +1 was always the human in the loop. Chapter 12 specifies *what the human in the loop is actually doing* across the year. The +1 is not a static role; it is a maintained practice.

**The chapter also reinforces the thesis by surfacing the *cost* of the discipline.** The practice takes time — modest amounts daily, more weekly and monthly. The chapter should not pretend this time is free. The argument is that the time spent maintaining the practice is the time that distinguishes the practitioner whose judgment compounds in value from the practitioner whose judgment atrophies. This is consistent with the Chapter 11 forecast that judgment-shaped work appreciates as pattern-shaped work commoditizes — but it requires that the practitioner *be doing the judgment work*, which requires the practice.

**The +1 in Chapter 12 is the practitioner committing to sustain the practice past the close of the book.** Every prior chapter prepared the reader to make a delegation call; Chapter 12 asks the reader to keep making those calls deliberately for years, under tool change, role change, capability change, and the pressure of busy quarters. The chapter must earn this commitment, not assume it.

**A risk to manage:** the chapter must avoid making the practice feel monastic, performative, or guilt-inducing. The honest framing is: pattern-shaped work is going to be delegated more, with or without the practice; the question is whether the practitioner directs the delegation or is directed by it. The practice is the practitioner directing it.

## 5. Wayback Candidates — Historical Figures

Three figures whose work models the sustained discipline of professional practice under technological pressure. Diverse where possible.

**Candidate A — Florence Nightingale (1820–1910), nurse, statistician, and reformer.**
Nightingale is famous as a nurse and almost no one outside statistics knows she was a foundational medical statistician — the first woman elected to the Royal Statistical Society (1858), and the inventor (or co-inventor) of the polar-area diagram (the "Nightingale rose"). *Why she fits Chapter 12:* Nightingale built and sustained a daily discipline of recording mortality data in the Crimean military hospitals, then used that data to reform sanitary practice across the British military and beyond. The practice she sustained was unglamorous, daily, and over years; the integration of measurement with judgment was the entire program. Chapter 12 is teaching the practitioner that the sustained, unglamorous daily record is what makes judgment defensible and calibration possible. Nightingale's discipline is the historical archetype. She also rebuilt her practice every time the technology of medicine changed — sanitation, germ theory, hospital architecture — and her stance was that the discipline of measurement and reflection survives any specific medical paradigm. Directly transferable to the AI+1 practice surviving any specific tool.

**Candidate B — Charles Darwin (1809–1882), naturalist — used here for his daily practice, not the famous theory.**
Darwin maintained a notebook practice — the famous "Notebook B" with the "I think" sketch and his many subsequent transmutation notebooks — for over 20 years before publishing *On the Origin of Species*. He recorded daily observations, contradictions, anomalies, and questions. His son Francis Darwin's biographical work and the modern scholarship (e.g., Janet Browne's two-volume biography) document the discipline. *Why he fits Chapter 12:* Darwin is the model of the practitioner whose daily practice of recording, reflecting, and re-classifying was what made the eventual judgment defensible. The slow accretion of observation into a working framework is exactly what the AI+1 delegation log is meant to produce in the practitioner's own work. The chapter can borrow Darwin's specific practice — a small daily entry, a weekly re-reading, a monthly synthesis — as direct precedent.

**Candidate C — Mae C. Jemison (b. 1956), physician, engineer, and the first Black woman to travel in space (NASA, 1992).**
Jemison's career arc — internal medicine, Peace Corps medical officer in West Africa, astronaut, founder of an engineering and technology consultancy, professor at Cornell, head of the 100 Year Starship initiative — is a sustained discipline of professional reinvention under technological change. She has written and spoken extensively about the practice of integrating new fields, new tools, and new domains across decades. *Why she fits Chapter 12:* the chapter's argument is partly that the AI+1 practice equips the practitioner for a career across capability cycles, not a job within one. Jemison is the contemporary model of that arc — someone whose practice of integration is the through-line across radically different technical contexts. She is also a representation choice: a Black woman whose professional discipline of cross-domain integration is the substance of her contribution, not an incidental fact about her biography.

**(Alternate, if a fourth is wanted:) Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790).** Franklin's *Autobiography* describes his 13-virtues weekly self-evaluation practice — a small daily/weekly record of moral and professional discipline. Probably too famous; cited here for completeness. Better used as a brief illustrative footnote than as a wayback anchor.

## 6. Pedagogy — How to Teach This

**Center the chapter on a small set of *rituals the reader will actually adopt*, not an exhaustive system.** The instructional-design literature is unambiguous: 70% of new practices fail within 90 days. The chapter must design for the 30% that survive by making the minimum viable practice cheap and the full practice optional.

**Start by asking the reader the honest question:** what will you actually do in 30 days, 90 days, a year? The chapter is more valuable if the reader walks away with a 15-minute-a-day practice they sustain than with a 90-minute-a-day practice they abandon in two weeks. Frame the practice as scalable: a minimum viable version that fits any week, and richer layers the reader can add when the work supports it.

**Make the practice anchored to existing routines (Fogg, Wood, Clear).** The daily delegation check happens at task-start, not as a separate ritual. The weekly review attaches to the existing weekly recap. The monthly map revision attaches to the monthly reporting cycle. The quarterly audit attaches to the quarterly planning cycle. This is *habit stacking*, made specific to social marketing's existing rhythm.

**Use implementation intentions explicitly (Gollwitzer).** Don't tell the reader "review your map weekly." Tell the reader "every Friday at the end of your team standup, open your delegation log and ask three questions: where did I over-delegate? where did I under-delegate? what surprised me?" The if-then specificity is what moves the practice from intention to behavior.

**Build the chapter around three artifacts the reader produces:**
1. **A one-page personal practice contract.** The reader writes down what minimum viable practice they commit to, on what cadence, anchored to what existing routines, reviewed when. Template provided. Signed and dated.
2. **A delegation log starter.** Format-agnostic (markdown, Notion, paper). The chapter provides a worked example from the first 30 days and a copy-paste prompt block to generate the format.
3. **A 90-day review prompt.** A specific set of questions the reader will ask themselves in 90 days, scheduled into their calendar at chapter end.

**Address the inevitable abandonment honestly.** Most readers will not sustain the practice. The chapter should make peace with this and design the structure so even a 30-day partial run leaves the reader with calibrated insight. The Tetlock research is clear: calibrated practice is teachable, and even partial training improves judgment. A reader who runs the practice for 30 days and then drops it is still better calibrated than a reader who never starts.

**Close the book with a deliberate handoff move.** The chapter has to send the reader forward as a practitioner. Possible structural moves:
- A statement of what the reader is now equipped to do that they were not before.
- A specific invitation: write down one delegation decision in the next 24 hours and email it to a colleague.
- A reading list / community list for continued development.
- The Claude Code prompt block that lets the reader generate their own first week of the practice today.

**Avoid these pedagogical traps:**
- Overcomplicating the daily practice. If it takes more than 15 minutes a day at minimum, it dies.
- Tool prescriptions. The chapter loses durability if it requires a specific app.
- Moralism. The chapter is not about being a *good* practitioner; it is about being a *calibrated* one.
- Closing on hype. The chapter ends with practice, not prophecy.

**End-of-chapter outputs:**
1. The reader's personal practice contract.
2. The reader's delegation log started.
3. The reader's first week scheduled.
4. The reader's 90-day review on the calendar.
5. The reader's named accountability partner or community.

## 7. Representation

**Practice traditions to draw from explicitly:** Western behavior design (Fogg, Wood, Clear); experiential learning (Kolb, Schön); deliberate practice (Ericsson, Macnamara); systems and operations (Drucker, Allen); Japanese kata and continuous improvement (Rother, Ohno); naturalistic observation (Darwin). Cross multiple traditions deliberately.

**Voices on professional discipline in changing fields:** Nightingale (medicine), Darwin (natural history), Jemison (medicine / engineering), Gawande (medicine — cross-reference Chapter 11), Drucker (management). Each is a sustained-practice voice; together they cross gender, era, and discipline.

**Practitioner archetypes named in the chapter so readers can find themselves:**
- The in-house social media manager at a mid-sized brand (the primary reader).
- The agency account lead working across multiple clients.
- The freelance / consulting marketer.
- The founder doing their own social.
- The marketing-ops practitioner integrating AI across the team.
- The internal trainer rolling out AI literacy at scale.

Each has a different version of the practice. The chapter should briefly walk each.

**Geographic and cultural frame:** the daily-practice literature is heavily Western and individualistic. The chapter should at minimum note that some readers operate in team-first or collective practice cultures where the discipline is shared rather than personal. The practice transfers; the framing may need to.

**Voice and tone of the chapter:** practitioner-to-practitioner, peer-level, time-respecting. Not a guru voice. Not a coach voice. Not a futurist voice. The chapter has earned the right to be direct because the prior 11 chapters did the work.

**Demographic representation in named examples:** maintain the same standard as Chapter 10. Specifically check that the practitioner archetypes named in the chapter are not all the same demographic. Worked examples should include a mix of agency / in-house / freelance / founder; gender-balanced where named individuals appear; geographically distributed where reasonable.

## 8. Open Questions

1. **What is the minimum viable practice?** Recommend: a daily 5-minute end-of-day delegation log entry plus a monthly 15-minute map check. Everything else is optional layering. Decision needed before drafting because the chapter's structure hinges on it.
2. **Does the chapter provide a software template?** The temptation is to publish a Notion template, an Obsidian vault, an Airtable base. Risk: ages in 12–18 months, ties the practice to a tool. Recommend: format-agnostic markdown template only, with one paragraph noting that the reader can implement in any tool.
3. **Is the AI+1 practice a *solo* practice or a *team* practice?** Recommend: foundationally solo (because the practitioner's judgment cannot be outsourced), with explicit team-level extensions. A two-page section on rolling the practice out to a team is enough; the chapter is not a change-management book.
4. **How prescriptive about cadence?** Recommend: prescriptive about *that* there is a cadence (daily / weekly / monthly / quarterly); flexible about specifics. Practitioners will adapt.
5. **Does the chapter close on a forecast?** Recommend: no. Chapter 11 handles forecasting. Chapter 12 closes on practice. Resist the temptation to grand-finale with prediction.
6. **What is the reader's affective state at the end of the book?** This is a deliberate craft question. Recommend: equipped, calibrated, and slightly humbled — not pumped up. The book's authority comes from sobriety, not enthusiasm. The close should match.
7. **Does the chapter recommend a community / further reading?** Recommend: yes — light. A short list of resources for continued development (Humanitarians AI workshops, the *Irreducibly Human* series, a small number of primary sources). Resist the urge to make this a "next steps" mini-syllabus.
8. **Should the chapter address compensation explicitly — "the practice is the path to compounding professional value"?** Recommend: yes, but briefly, in the Thesis Connection rather than as the chapter's main argument. The practice is justified on its own terms; compensation is a consequence, not the motivation.
9. **What happens when the practitioner gets it wrong in the field — over-delegates and the brand takes damage?** The chapter should have a section on recovery: what to do after a failed delegation call. This is the maintenance literature's "lapse vs. relapse" distinction. One lapse does not end a practice; the recovery protocol does.
10. **What is the chapter's stance on AI tooling for the practice itself?** (i.e., should the practitioner use Claude to draft their weekly review?) Recommend: yes for first draft of reflection, but with an explicit caveat — the reflection itself is judgment work, so the human edit is mandatory. The practice models the practice it teaches.

## 9. Sourcing Notes

**Aging risk by source class (high to low):**
- Specific tool names and product capabilities (Notion AI, ChatGPT custom GPTs, Claude Projects, specific vendor enterprise features): very high. Use only as illustrative examples with date stamps; prefer category-level language.
- Industry survey adoption / abandonment statistics: medium. Cite the most recent at writing; do not anchor an argument to a single year's number.
- Behavior change literature foundational works (Fogg, Wood, Gollwitzer, Schön, Drucker, Ericsson, Allen): low. These are the chapter's spine.
- Specific replication concerns (ego depletion, marshmallow test, original 21-day habit claim): citable with the corrective literature.
- Historical figures (Nightingale, Darwin, Jemison): low. Use standard biographical sources (Bostridge on Nightingale; Browne on Darwin; Jemison's own *Find Where the Wind Goes* and her recent NASA / 100 Year Starship interviews).

**Verification rhythm:**
- Tool examples: verify within 30 days of submission. Note in prose where the example is illustrative.
- Behavior change effect sizes: cite the original paper and any meta-analysis or replication note. Use ranges, not point estimates.
- Historical claims: cite biography of record, not Wikipedia.
- Practitioner archetypes: verify against current job titles and role definitions in major social marketing industry sources (CMI, MarketingProfs, AMA) within drafting window.

**Citation conventions:**
- Behavior change / habit literature: primary paper + accessible synthesis. Note where the popular version diverges from the empirical claim (Lally et al. 66-day median is the most-misquoted example).
- Workflow / practice literature: cite by book and chapter; many of these works have specific load-bearing chapters.
- Historical figures: cite biography of record.
- Vendor / platform documentation: cite by document title, version, and date accessed. Treat as illustrative.

**Things the chapter should deliberately *not* source:**
- Productivity-influencer claims about specific daily routines as universally optimal. The chapter is opinionated about cadence-and-structure; it should not be opinionated about wake times, morning rituals, or specific journal formats.
- Specific dollar-value ROI claims for AI workflow integration. The literature is noisy and motivated; the chapter's argument does not depend on a specific number.
- Prescriptive lists of which AI tools to use this year. Categories only.

**The chapter's own aging strategy:**
- The *practice structure* (daily / weekly / monthly / quarterly cadence with named outputs) is durable.
- The *specific tool examples* are illustrative and replaceable.
- The *behavior change scaffolding* (anchor + cue + tiny behavior + reflection) is durable.
- The *worked-example practitioner journey* is the most refresh-able element; reprint editions can update the 30-day case study without touching the chapter's spine.

**A specific drafting note:** the chapter must include a worked example of one practitioner's 30-day practice — what they wrote in their delegation log, what they noticed, what they revised. This is the load-bearing piece of pedagogy. Recommend constructing it as a composite-and-labeled-as-such if necessary, or basing it on a documented case study with permission. Do not invent a named practitioner. Composite or named-and-consented or labeled-hypothetical — those are the three honest options.

**The closing pages of the book are a craft decision more than a research decision.** What research can offer: the transfer literature is unambiguous that the close should re-state the load-bearing claim, hand the reader a specific next action, and invite the reader into ongoing practice. The chapter (and the book) ends with the reader writing in the delegation log for the first time, not with the author summarizing the thesis. Earn the close by handing it over.
