# Research: Chapter 01 — The Boundary
## Social Media Marketing +1
**Chapter one-line:** Why AI+1, what's at stake in both directions (over-delegating vs. under-using).
**Research date:** 2026-05-27

---

## 1. Primary Sources

### Foundational papers and texts

1. **Daugherty, P. & Wilson, H. J. (2018).** *Human + Machine: Reimagining Work in the Age of AI.* Harvard Business Review Press.
   The original "missing middle" formulation — humans and machines complementing rather than substituting. Daugherty's argument that the productivity gains live in the boundary, not on either side of it, is the structural ancestor of "AI+1." For Chapter 1, this is the source that establishes that the question "what should I delegate?" is a real management question, not a tooling question.

2. **Brynjolfsson, E. (2022).** "The Turing Trap: The Promise and Peril of Human-Like AI." *Daedalus,* 151(2), 272–287.
   Brynjolfsson argues that designing AI to imitate humans (rather than augment them) systematically misallocates value — the gains accrue to capital, not labor, and the work itself degrades. The chapter's "under-using" failure mode (treating AI as a person, getting human-shaped slop) and "over-delegating" failure mode (handing off what only judgment can do) are both Turing Trap symptoms.

3. **Raisch, S. & Krakowski, S. (2021).** "Artificial Intelligence and Management: The Automation–Augmentation Paradox." *Academy of Management Review,* 46(1), 192–210.
   Names the paradox at the center of Chapter 1: every automation decision quietly removes a site where human judgment used to live, and the cost of that removal is invisible until something goes wrong. The "two directions" framing (over-delegate, under-use) comes directly from this paradox.

4. **Autor, D. (2015).** "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? The History and Future of Workplace Automation." *Journal of Economic Perspectives,* 29(3), 3–30.
   Autor's "O-ring" argument: in a workflow with multiple steps, automating most of them only raises the value of the steps that resist automation. For social media marketing, the un-automatable steps (brand voice calibration, crisis judgment, relationship management) become more, not less, valuable as drafting and scheduling are delegated. This is the economic spine of the "+1."

5. **Polanyi, M. (1966).** *The Tacit Dimension.* University of Chicago Press.
   "We can know more than we can tell." The foundational philosophical claim that there is a category of professional knowledge — taste, judgment, fit — that resists articulation and therefore resists training data. The Guard List exists because Polanyi was right.

### Key empirical cases

1. **Sports Illustrated AI-author scandal (November 2023, documented by Futurism).** SI published articles under fake AI-generated author bylines with AI headshots; readership and advertiser trust collapsed when discovered; the licensing deal ended weeks later. Clean illustration of over-delegation eroding the human signal that made the brand worth paying for.

2. **CNET AI-written finance articles (January 2023, documented by The Verge and Futurism).** CNET quietly published 77 AI-drafted financial advice articles; later corrections covered more than half. The pattern-shaped work (drafting) was delegated; the judgment-shaped work (does this financial advice hold up?) was not retained. Audience trust dropped measurably; the site issued a public reckoning.

3. **Coca-Cola "Create Real Magic" campaign (2023, documented by Coca-Cola press releases and Adweek coverage).** The under-using counterexample: Coke used generative AI for variation and ideation at scale, but retained human creative direction over which outputs ran and how the brand voice was framed. Reported as a directional success — useful as a "what the boundary looks like when it holds" example, labeled as company-reported.

## 2. The Core Concept — State of the Field

### What is settled

- Generative AI substantially improves productivity on well-specified writing tasks. (Noy & Zhang, *Science,* 2023; Brynjolfsson, Li, & Raymond, NBER 2023, customer support study.)
- AI-generated text is detectable by audiences at rates above chance when context is high-stakes or brand-distinctive. (Kreps et al., 2022; Jakesch et al., 2023.)
- AI tools fail silently on judgment calls — they produce confident, plausible output regardless of whether the underlying decision is correct. (Bender et al., "Stochastic Parrots," 2021; Bommasani et al., Stanford CRFM, 2021.)
- Over-reliance degrades operator skill over time ("automation complacency," well documented in aviation and clinical contexts; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Early studies of GenAI in knowledge work suggest the same pattern.

### What is disputed

- Whether the judgment/pattern boundary is stable or transient. Some (e.g., Altman, Amodei in public talks) argue it migrates as models improve; others (e.g., Bender, Mitchell, Marcus) argue some categories are structurally out of reach.
- Whether AI-assisted content harms or helps engagement metrics. Platform-side studies (Meta, LinkedIn) report neutral-to-positive engagement; independent audits (NewsGuard, Stanford Internet Observatory) report engagement collapse when AI provenance becomes visible.
- Whether disclosure of AI use should be mandatory. EU AI Act (2024) requires it for some categories; FTC guidance (2024) is softer; platform policies (TikTok, Meta) require disclosure for synthetic media but not for AI-assisted text.

### What has changed recently (last 5 years)

- The pattern-shaped tier has expanded fast: scheduling, captioning, hashtag research, thumbnail variation, A/B copy generation, basic analytics summarization are all routinely delegated now where they were not in 2021.
- The judgment-shaped tier has held — brand voice, community trust, crisis response, partnership decisions, audience reading — but the *appearance* of judgment-shaped work has been blurred by better models. The Chapter 1 problem is real-time: the boundary is harder to see now than it was, but no less consequential.
- The cost of under-using has risen sharply. A team that does pattern work manually in 2026 is structurally slower than competitors and burns judgment hours on things judgment doesn't help with.
- The cost of over-delegating has also risen — audiences are more sensitive to "AI smell" than they were in 2023, and platform algorithms are starting to demote it (TikTok's 2024 originality signals, LinkedIn's 2025 ranking changes).

## 3. Application Domain Examples

1. **Caption drafting at a mid-size B2B SaaS company.** A solo social manager drafts 30 LinkedIn captions per week. Delegating first drafts to AI, then editing for voice, recovers 6–8 hours weekly. Pure Delegate case. Failure mode if the manager skips editing: posts read as generic, engagement drops within 4–6 weeks.

2. **Crisis response after a product outage at a consumer fintech.** Within 90 minutes, the social team must post acknowledgment that's accurate, compassionate, legally vetted, and tonally calibrated to a frustrated user base. Pure Guard case. AI can summarize the incoming volume; AI cannot decide what to say or whether to say it now.

3. **Community management for a Discord-anchored creator brand.** Routing — "this is a refund question, this is a product question, this is a troll" — can be partially delegated to AI classification. Response itself, particularly to the troll, cannot. Mistake mode: a brand that lets AI auto-respond to community members and treats them like ticketed support kills the community it was trying to manage.

4. **Influencer partnership outreach at a beauty brand.** Initial research (audience overlap, prior brand collaborations, post cadence) is delegable. Decision to partner and the first relational outreach email is not. The Sports Illustrated lesson: bylines and relationships are the same kind of thing — both are the brand's promise that a human is on the other end.

5. **Analytics interpretation at a DTC apparel brand.** Pulling, joining, and summarizing metrics across platforms is Delegate. Deciding what the metric drop on Tuesday *meant* — campaign fatigue, algorithm change, real audience shift — is Guard. The pattern of the data is what AI sees; the interpretation in context is what the human owes.

## 4. The Book's Thesis Connection

Chapter 1 is the chapter that names the thesis. Everything after this is application; this is where the reader has to accept the frame.

The chapter does three thesis-specific moves:

- **It names the boundary as real, not aspirational.** Not "AI might one day be unable to do X." AI, today, in the configuration the reader has access to, executes patterns and does not exercise judgment. This is empirical, not philosophical — and the chapter has to hold it as empirical so that Chapter 2's Delegate/Guard List has teeth.

- **It names the cost in both directions.** The thesis fails if it sounds like Luddism ("don't use AI, you'll lose your soul") or like maximalism ("use AI for everything, the boundary will move"). Chapter 1's job is the symmetric story: over-delegating erodes the human signal that made the marketing work; under-using burns the judgment hours on pattern work and leaves the manager structurally slower than competitors. Both failures are real, both are common, and the AI+1 frame is the only one that addresses both.

- **It commits to "currently requires" as the verbal posture.** The thesis is not "humans are special forever." The thesis is "as of today, judgment-shaped work currently requires a human, and you are running a brand today." This framing protects the book from aging — even when the boundary moves, the practice of drawing it doesn't.

The chapter's opening case must make the boundary visible in a single example — not argue for it. The arguing happens after the reader has already seen the cost.

## 5. The AI Wayback Machine — Candidate Figures

1. **Lillian Gilbreth (1878–1972).** Industrial psychologist and engineer; co-developed time-and-motion studies but, against Taylor, insisted that the worker's judgment and fatigue were data, not noise. She is the figure who first argued that a workflow has both pattern-shaped steps (reducible, measurable, optimizable) and judgment-shaped steps (irreducible, contextual, human) — and that ignoring the second category breaks the first. Lesser-known relative to Taylor, woman, accessible Wikipedia page, directly relevant.
   *Anchor prompt example:* "Lillian Gilbreth in 1925 watching a bricklayer. She measures the motions she can measure. She refuses to measure the judgment call about which bricks the wall actually needs. What does she see that Taylor missed?"

2. **Norbert Wiener (1894–1964).** Mathematician, founder of cybernetics; in *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950) argued that the question of automation is not "can the machine do it" but "what does the human owe the system that the machine cannot owe." Wiener's framing of the human as the accountable node is the philosophical ancestor of the Guard List. Mid-tier known, white male — counterbalance with Gilbreth or a third candidate.
   *Anchor prompt example:* "Norbert Wiener, 1950, writing about the first industrial robots. He is not worried about whether they can lift. He is worried about who is responsible when they drop something. What does he tell the factory owner?"

3. **Grace Hopper (1906–1992).** Computer scientist; her insistence on COBOL — a programming language readable by non-programmers — was a fight against the over-delegation of judgment to specialists. Her contribution to Chapter 1 is the principle that abstractions which hide the judgment layer make the judgment layer harder to defend. The argument for keeping a human "+1" is the same shape as her argument for keeping code readable. Woman, well-known but the specific judgment-vs-abstraction angle is fresh.
   *Anchor prompt example:* "Grace Hopper in 1959 arguing that programming languages should be readable by accountants. Why? Because the accountant has to be able to see whether the computer is doing the right thing. What is she protecting?"

Diversity balance: one woman (Gilbreth), one man (Wiener), one woman (Hopper). All Western — future chapters should counterbalance with non-Western candidates. Chapter 1 candidates are deliberately "boundary thinkers" rather than "AI history" figures — this matches the chapter's job of naming the boundary, not the technology.

## 6. Pedagogical Delivery Research

**Prior knowledge the reader brings.** The reader has used ChatGPT, Claude, Jasper, Copy.ai, or similar tools for at least drafting captions or generating ideas. They have likely read at least one "ChatGPT for Marketing" prompt book. They have a working mental model of "prompt → output" but not of "task type → delegation decision."

**Misconceptions to surface and correct early.**
1. *"More AI = better output."* This is the first thing the chapter must puncture, ideally with the Sports Illustrated or CNET case. The volume of AI use does not predict the quality of the marketing; the *fit* of AI use to task type does.
2. *"The human layer is editing."* Editing is pattern work. Judgment is asking whether the post should be made at all, whether the angle is right, whether the moment is right. Many readers conflate "I edited the output" with "I exercised judgment." Chapter 1 has to separate the two.
3. *"AI tools will tell me when they're wrong."* They do not. They produce confident text regardless of whether the underlying decision is sound. This is not a bug to be fixed in the next model release — it is structural. The Guard List exists because of this.

**Instructional sequence that works for Chapter 1.**
- Open with a case where the boundary failed visibly (over-delegation).
- Name the symmetric case where under-using also costs (a manager doing scheduling and hashtag research manually).
- Introduce pattern-shaped vs. judgment-shaped as a vocabulary, not a taxonomy. The taxonomy comes in Chapter 2.
- Close with the reader's own diagnostic: where on your workflow are you currently failing in each direction?

**Failure modes in teaching this chapter.**
- *Sounding anti-AI.* If the chapter reads as "AI is dangerous, be careful," the practitioner reader bounces. They already use the tools; they are looking for a framework, not a warning.
- *Sounding pro-AI.* If the chapter reads as "AI is amazing, here's how to use it more," it collapses into the prompt-library competitor set.
- *Naming the boundary too crisply.* If the boundary sounds like a clean checklist in Chapter 1, the reader gets a false confidence. Chapter 1 should name the boundary as real but acknowledge it is *learned through cases,* which is what Chapters 3–9 then do.

## 7. Representation and Display Research

Chapter 1 is a conceptual chapter — it introduces the boundary, it does not yet operationalize it. By the chapter anatomy (8-part structure), it still requires:

- A short Delegate List preview (1–2 lines) showing what's coming.
- A short Guard List preview (1–2 lines) showing what's coming.
- A "Do This with AI" section, even at this conceptual level — likely a single concrete instruction such as "list every social marketing task you did this week and circle the ones where you used judgment, not pattern recognition."
- A "Never Do This with AI" section — likely "don't outsource the decision about what to post when something has gone wrong."
- A copy-paste Claude Code prompt at chapter end. For Chapter 1 the natural prompt is a self-audit: have Claude help the reader build a first-pass list of their own pattern-shaped vs. judgment-shaped tasks.

No full Delegate List / Guard List table is required in Chapter 1 — that is Chapter 2's job. But the chapter should display the *anatomy* the rest of the book will follow, so the reader recognizes the rhythm by Chapter 3.

## 8. Open Questions and Research Gaps

- **Empirical engagement data on AI-assisted vs. human-written social content is contested.** Platforms report neutral effects; independent researchers report negative effects when AI is detectable. The chapter should reference both rather than choose.
- **The "automation complacency" literature is strong in aviation and clinical contexts but thin in marketing.** Direct transfer is suggestive, not proven. Flag.
- **Sources likely outdated within 3 years:** platform-specific examples (TikTok originality signals, LinkedIn ranking changes), model-specific examples (any reference to specific Claude or GPT versions). Use "as of 2026" framing.
- **Disputed claim flagged:** the assertion that audiences detect AI-generated text "at rates above chance" is true on average but heterogeneous — varies by domain, by audience expertise, by length. State as "in studied conditions" rather than universally.
