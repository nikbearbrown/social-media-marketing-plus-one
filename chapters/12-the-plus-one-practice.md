# Chapter 12 — The +1 Practice

*Integration, workflow, daily discipline*

---

## Opening case

A social media manager at a mid-size DTC skincare brand — call her Priya — finished a four-week internal AI training in February. She walked out with a 12-page Delegate/Guard map she had built herself, a prompt library tied to it, a calendar invite for a weekly review, and the genuine intention to maintain the practice. Composite case; the pattern is documented across multiple 2024–2025 marketing workforce surveys.

By April, two things had happened. The first: a 30% time savings on captions, paid copy variants, and the monthly recap deck. Measurable. Real. The second: the weekly review had not occurred since week three. The delegation log had four entries from the first week and none after. Priya had not made a deliberate Delegate-versus-Guard call in roughly eight weeks; she had defaulted to whatever was fast in the moment. When her director asked her in May why a paid LinkedIn post had gone out with a competitor's product name in the body copy — clearly an AI hallucination she would have caught two months earlier — she could not answer.

This is not a story about AI failure. The tool worked exactly as designed. It is a story about practice failure. The empirical reality, documented in Salesforce, Microsoft, and McKinsey workforce surveys 2023–2025, is that most workers adopt AI tools, stabilize on a smaller core use case, and then drift — losing the discipline that distinguished calibrated delegation from reflex delegation. [verify against most recent figures] The 70% practice-abandonment rate inside 90 days that the behavior-change literature reports for new professional habits applies here too. [verify — Norcross et al. on resolution maintenance; figure approximate]

This chapter is about the practice that survives the drift. Not the maximal version. The version that fits in 15 minutes a day, anchors to routines you already have, and produces a longitudinal record of your own delegation calls that compounds in value across years. The honest framing: you will not do the elaborate version. You might do the small one. The small one, sustained, is the +1.

## The pattern-shaped work in this task (Delegate List)

The practice itself is partly patterned. AI can help with the pieces below — but the practice is meaningfully done only when the human is in the loop on reflection.

1. **Log formatting.** AI can take your raw end-of-day notes and structure them into a delegation log entry with consistent fields (task, delegate/guard call, surprise, what-I'd-do-differently).
2. **Pattern detection across log entries.** Given a month of log entries, AI can surface recurring over-delegation, under-delegation, or boundary-case patterns. You decide what they mean.
3. **Weekly review summary draft.** Given the week's log, AI can draft the three-question recap (where did I over-delegate, where did I under-delegate, what surprised me) for you to revise.
4. **Brand-voice sample comparison.** Given a baseline set of pre-AI brand posts and this week's AI-touched output, AI can run a structured comparison surfacing voice drift candidates for you to audit.
5. **Prompt-library maintenance reminders.** AI can flag prompt templates that haven't been tested in 60+ days, that reference deprecated tool versions, or that produced inconsistent outputs across runs.
6. **Capability-watch summarization.** Given your watchlist from Chapter 11, AI can produce monthly digests of vendor disclosures and evaluation reports.
7. **Personal practice contract drafts.** AI can draft the one-page commitment document you'll sign, based on your stated minimum viable practice and your existing calendar rhythm.
8. **Onboarding documentation for team rollouts.** If you extend the practice to a team, AI can draft the operating-rhythm document, the role-specific log templates, and the calibration meeting agenda.

## The judgment-shaped work in this task (Guard List)

The practice currently requires a human for the following:

1. **The reflection itself.** AI can format your reflection; only you can do it. Reflection-on-action — Donald Schön's term in *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983) — is the thing that produces calibration. Outsourcing the reflection is exactly outsourcing the calibration.
2. **The honest delegation log entry.** "I delegated this because I was tired, not because the task was pattern-shaped" is the kind of entry that makes the log valuable. AI cannot write that for you. It can only write the entry you would write if you were performing.
3. **The voice-drift judgment.** The weekly brand-voice audit asks whether AI-touched content is converging on a flattened voice. AI is exactly the system whose drift you are auditing; it is not the auditor.
4. **The boundary-case stance.** When a task that has been on Guard surfaces a real opportunity to move to Delegate, the call is yours. The forecast register from Chapter 11 informs the call; the call itself is a commitment with downside.
5. **The recovery from a failed delegation.** When an AI-touched output goes out and damages the brand, the response — apology, correction, escalation, root-cause analysis, map revision — is human-led. The recovery protocol is one of the load-bearing items of the practice.
6. **The decision to sustain or revise the practice itself.** Quarterly, you ask: is the practice serving the work, serving the brand, or feeling performative? The answer shapes the next quarter. Only you can answer.
7. **The accountability partner relationship.** Practices that survive tend to have a peer or small group. The relationship — who you trust to read your log, who you check in with, who you let see the embarrassing entry — is irreducibly human.
8. **The career-arc judgment.** Three years from now, the longitudinal record of your own delegation calls is a professional artifact. Whether you treat it that way, present it as evidence in performance reviews, write about it publicly, use it as the basis for your next role — these are calls only you can make.

## Do This with AI — the minimum viable practice

The chapter's central commitment: a 15-minute-a-day practice, scalable up to a quarterly half-day audit, anchored to routines you already have. Florence Nightingale's daily mortality logs in the Crimean military hospitals were unglamorous, repetitive, and over years; that is the model. Charles Darwin's transmutation notebooks ran for 20+ years of small daily entries that became the working framework. Mae Jemison built a career arc of cross-domain integration on practice, not on prophecy. The discipline is old; the application is current.

**Daily (5–10 min total).**

- **Morning anchor (2 min).** Open the Delegate/Guard map. Note which task families will be exercised today. Note any boundary cases that have surfaced in the last 24 hours.
- **Per-task delegation check (in-flow, under 30 seconds per task).** Before starting a social marketing task, classify it: Delegate / Guard / boundary. Apply the relevant Do This / Never Do This. This is the implementation intention (Gollwitzer, 1999) that prevents drift — *when* I start a task, *then* I classify it first.
- **End-of-day delegation log (3–5 min).** Note the three to five most consequential delegation calls of the day. Format-agnostic: markdown, Notion, paper. What was delegated, what was guarded, what surprised you, what you'd do differently. Five sentences is sufficient.

**Weekly (15–30 min, attached to your existing weekly recap).**

- **Log scan (10 min).** Read the week's entries. Mark recurring over-delegation, under-delegation, boundary-case clusters, tool failures. Update the map if a pattern is clear.
- **Brand-voice audit (10 min).** Sample 5–10 pieces of content the brand produced this week. Read them as the audience. Note voice drift. This is the human-only audit of AI-touched output.
- **Tool calibration (5 min).** Note any change in tool capability — a model upgrade, a new feature, a new failure mode. Update one or two prompt templates if needed.

**Monthly (45–60 min, attached to your monthly reporting cycle).**

- **Map revision.** Move items between Delegate and Guard if the data supports it. Document the move and the reasoning.
- **Calibration check.** Pick five outputs from the past month. For each: did the AI+1 practice produce a better output than no-AI would have? Than full-AI would have? Honestly. The honest answer is the calibration.
- **Forecast register touch.** Pull the Chapter 11 register. Re-score any forecast whose horizon is approaching. Note new capability developments worth tracking.

**Quarterly (3–4 hours, attached to your quarterly planning cycle).**

- **Full audit.** Re-run the Chapter 10 audit protocol. Has the map drifted? Has the org context changed? Have new task families emerged? Have regulatory conditions changed?
- **Practice retrospective.** Three honest questions: where did the practice serve the work? Where did the practice serve the brand? Where did the practice feel performative?
- **Forward calibration.** What is the next quarter's experiment? Document one specific delegation experiment to run.

**The minimum-viable version, if you will do nothing else.** End-of-day delegation log, five sentences, five days a week. Monthly 15-minute map check on the last Friday of the month. That is it. Everything above is optional layering. The Lally et al. *European Journal of Social Psychology* (2009) finding — that habit formation has wide individual variance around a 66-day median — argues for low-friction starts that survive the bad weeks. [verify]

**Worked example: Priya's first 30 days, take two.**

Week 1, Monday: opened a fresh markdown file titled `delegation-log.md`. Logged three calls — one Delegate (paid copy variants, fine), one Guard (response to a customer in DMs with a service complaint, kept human), one boundary case (a comment thread on an Instagram post where AI-drafted moderator language might have worked but felt risky). Five sentences total. Closed the laptop.

Week 1, Friday weekly review: noticed she had logged six entries in the boundary-case category and only one each in Delegate and Guard. Realized she was using "boundary" as a way to avoid making the call. Marked the pattern.

Week 2: forced herself to classify boundary cases by end of day Tuesday. Discovered three were actually Delegate and two were actually Guard; she had been hedging. Map revised on Friday — two prompts added to the prompt library; one Guard List item reinforced (no AI-drafted moderator language without a human in the loop, with a written reason).

Week 3: brand-voice audit caught a phrase that had appeared in four AI-drafted captions and was not in the brand's documented voice. Flagged as voice drift candidate. Adjusted the caption prompt template.

Week 4: monthly check. Reviewed log. Realized she had skipped Tuesday and Wednesday end-of-day entries during a launch week. Did not start over. Wrote a one-line note: "skip pattern: launch weeks; recovery: log on Thursday morning catching up." No moralism. Just data.

Day 30: practice had survived a launch. The log was 18 entries, not 20. The map had two real revisions. The voice audit had caught one drift. This is what a sustained practice looks like in actual conditions.

## Never Do This with AI

Failure modes specifically about the practice.

1. **Overcomplicating the daily ritual.** If it takes more than 15 minutes a day at minimum, it dies. The Wood and Lally literature is unambiguous: high-friction habits don't survive bad weeks. Design for the bad week.
2. **Tying the practice to a specific tool.** Notion is a tool. Obsidian is a tool. ChatGPT is a tool. Tools change. The practice is the discipline of writing the log, scanning the log, revising the map. The tool is the cheapest replaceable part. If you cannot port the practice in a week, you have built tool dependency, not practice.
3. **Outsourcing the reflection itself.** Asking AI to write your weekly review for you is asking AI to be calibrated on your behalf. It cannot be. AI can format your reflection; you have to do the reflection. The chapter is willing to be didactic on this point.
4. **Moralism.** The chapter is not about being a *good* practitioner; it is about being a *calibrated* one. If you skip a week, log the skip and continue. If you over-delegate under deadline pressure, log it. The Prochaska-DiClemente literature on behavior maintenance distinguishes *lapse* from *relapse* — one missed entry is a lapse, abandoning the practice is a relapse, and treating every lapse as a moral failure is what produces relapse. [verify]
5. **Treating the practice as performance.** A delegation log written for someone else to admire is not the log that produces calibration. The honest entry — including "I was lazy today, I let AI write something I should have drafted, here's what I'd change" — is the entry with information. If the log is not safe enough to be honest in, it is not yet your practice.
6. **Closing on prophecy.** It is tempting, in a final chapter, to predict what the field looks like in five years. Chapter 11 made the falsifiable forecasts on the record. Chapter 12 closes on the practice. The practice is the answer to whatever the field looks like in five years, because the practice is what makes you a practitioner who can re-calibrate when the field changes.
7. **Skipping the recovery protocol.** When an AI-touched output damages the brand, the next move is documented recovery: apology, correction, escalation, root cause, map revision. Without the recovery protocol, every failure becomes a moral indictment of the practice rather than the data point it is.
8. **Practicing alone forever.** Solitary practice is sustainable for some; for most, an accountability partner — a peer at another company, a colleague on a different team, a Humanitarians AI workshop cohort — keeps the practice alive past the third bad month.

## The +1

In every previous chapter, the +1 was the human review of a specific AI output. In Chapter 12, the +1 steps up one more level: you become the *sustained practitioner* of your own delegation system across years. Not for the duration of a campaign. Not for the length of this book. Across capability cycles, tool changes, role changes, and the pressure of busy quarters.

What you bring that AI cannot:

- **Continuity.** The longitudinal record of your own calls. A model has a context window; you have a career.
- **Honest reflection.** The willingness to write the entry that costs you something to admit. AI is structurally incapable of being honest in this register because it has nothing at stake.
- **Calibration to your own track record.** Reviewing your old log entries and noticing that you systematically over-delegated under deadline pressure, or systematically under-delegated on a particular task family. That self-knowledge compounds.
- **The decision to sustain.** The practice continues because you continue it. There is no automated version of choosing, today, to do the small unglamorous thing again.
- **The relationship layer.** Your accountability partner. Your team. Your peers. The practice is more durable when it is shared with at least one other human; that human is not a model.

**The argument for sustaining the practice, in plain terms.** Pattern-shaped work in social marketing is going to compress whether you participate or not. The trajectory is not in your hands. What is in your hands is whether you become the practitioner who directs the delegation or the practitioner who is directed by it. Chapter 11's forecast — that judgment-shaped work appreciates as pattern-shaped work commoditizes — depends on the practitioner actually doing the judgment work. The practice is doing the judgment work. Without the practice, the forecast does not apply to you.

**A note on the affective register.** The chapter does not promise mastery, productivity multipliers, or career insulation. It promises that the small discipline of recording your delegation calls, reviewing them honestly on a rhythm, and revising your map when the data supports it will, over years, produce a practitioner with calibrated judgment about AI in social marketing. That practitioner will be more valuable, more durable, and more sane than the practitioner who does not. The chapter is willing to be sober about this. Sobriety is the tone the book has earned.

**Closing the book.** This is the last page of the framework portion. There are three things to take from it.

First: you have read about the AI+1 framework. You now know the Delegate/Guard distinction, the Do This / Never Do This anatomy, the +1 as the judgment layer. Knowing the framework is not the practice.

Second: you have, if you ran the Chapter 10 audit, a first draft of your own map. If you have not run it yet, the next 24 hours is when the book either becomes your practice or becomes a book you read. The honest design of this chapter is to ask: write one delegation log entry today. Five sentences. End of day. That is the practice starting.

Third: the framework predicts that the practitioner who does the work compounds value across years. The forecast is falsifiable; the practice is the only way the forecast applies to you.

The book ends here. The practice begins where you start the log.

## Key terms

**Minimum viable practice.** The smallest version of the AI+1 practice that still produces calibration — a daily 5-sentence delegation log plus a monthly 15-minute map check. Designed to survive bad weeks. Everything above this is optional layering.

**Delegation log.** A maintained record of consequential delegation calls — what was delegated, what was guarded, what surprised you, what you'd do differently. Format-agnostic (markdown, Notion, paper). The discipline of writing it is what matters.

**Implementation intention.** Peter Gollwitzer's term (*American Psychologist*, 1999) for if-then plans that specify the cue, the behavior, and the moment. "When I start a social task, then I classify it as Delegate / Guard / boundary first." Effect sizes are robust; the chapter relies on the literature.

**Habit stacking.** Anchoring a new behavior to an existing routine (BJ Fogg's *Tiny Habits*, 2019; James Clear's *Atomic Habits*, 2018). The daily delegation log attaches to end-of-day shutdown; the weekly review attaches to the existing weekly recap. Reduces the activation cost of the practice.

**Reflection-on-action.** Donald Schön's term (*The Reflective Practitioner*, 1983) for the post-event review that turns experience into calibrated judgment. The weekly review and the monthly calibration check are reflection-on-action made operational. The reflection cannot be outsourced.

**Lapse versus relapse.** Distinction from the Prochaska-DiClemente Transtheoretical Model of behavior change. A lapse is a single missed entry; a relapse is abandoning the practice. Recovery protocols treat the lapse as data, not failure, and prevent the slide into relapse. [verify framing against most recent source]

**Recovery protocol.** The documented sequence for what to do when an AI-touched output damages the brand: apology, correction, escalation, root-cause analysis, map revision. Pre-written so it can be executed under pressure.

**Longitudinal record.** Your own delegation log accumulated across years. Becomes a professional artifact — evidence of calibration, adaptation, and judgment — that AI cannot produce on your behalf because it does not have your career or your accountability.
