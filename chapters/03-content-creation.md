# Chapter 3 — Content Creation

*Drafting, variation, ideation are pattern-shaped. Voice, angle, originality are judgment-shaped. Where the framework first meets the work practitioners do most.*

---

## Opening case

In 2022, Heinz prompted DALL-E with the single word "ketchup" and watched the model return image after image of bottles that looked like Heinz. The model had been trained on the internet's accumulated image of ketchup, and the internet's image of ketchup was Heinz. The marketing team turned this into a campaign — "Even AI knows the most iconic ketchup is Heinz" — and ran it across social and digital channels. Ad Age covered it as the canonical early example of generative AI used well in brand marketing. The *combinatorial* output of AI was the creative material. The *judgment* that this was the campaign was human.

A year later, in November 2023, *Sports Illustrated* published product reviews under bylines that did not exist, with AI headshots, until *Futurism* reporters found the headshots for sale on an AI stock-image marketplace. The articles read fine at the sentence level. The failure was not in the prose. It was in the byline. The contract the magazine had with its readers — that a person with this name and face had used this volleyball — turned out to be synthetic. The contract collapsed. The licensing deal between Arena Group and the SI brand ended within weeks.

Two cases. Same technology. One brand used AI for variation under human creative direction and got a campaign. One brand used AI to fabricate identity and lost the brand. The difference was where the boundary was drawn.

Content creation is the task most social media practitioners default to delegating, because drafting is the most visible time sink and AI is genuinely good at it. The Noy and Zhang study published in *Science* in 2023 found that ChatGPT cut average writing time by 40% and raised average quality by 18% on mid-skill writing tasks; the spread narrowed, meaning the worst writers improved more than the best. The productivity case is real. Pretending otherwise is dishonest.

But content creation is also the task where the over-delegation failure mode is most visible — at the byline level, at the voice level, and over time at the feed level. Doshi and Hauser's *Science Advances* paper in 2024 found that AI-assisted writers individually produced more creative stories on average, but the *collective set* of stories converged. Fewer distinct ideas across writers using the same model. For social media marketing, that finding is the structural risk: every brand using the same models drifts toward the same voice. This chapter's Guard List exists because of that finding.

The job of Chapter 3 is to draw the boundary inside content creation. Drafting and variation are Delegate. Voice and angle are Guard. Originality is feed-level, not post-level, and the Guard List has to protect the feed.

---

## The pattern-shaped work in this task (Delegate List)

What AI in 2026 reliably does well in content creation:

1. **First-draft prose from a brief.** Given audience, format, length, angle, and tone, current models produce captions, threads, carousel scripts, and short-form video scripts at draft quality that an editing human can finish.

2. **Variation generation.** Given an approved core message, producing ten or twenty rephrasings for A/B testing or platform-specific adaptation is what these systems are built for. Variation is the model's strongest move.

3. **Format adaptation.** Taking a long-form piece and compressing it into a thread; taking a thread and expanding into an essay; taking a script and rewriting it for a different platform's conventions.

4. **Ideation brainstorms (bounded).** Given a campaign theme or content pillar, producing twenty candidate angles for a human to select among. The selection is judgment; the production of candidates is pattern-shaped.

5. **Hashtag and tag research.** Surfacing what is currently trending in adjacent verticals, what cluster of tags is overused, what is performing in your specific space.

6. **Alt-text generation.** Description-pattern work; accessibility standards are teachable; volume requirements are real.

7. **Repost and quote-post copy.** Given the source, producing a frame that fits your brand's posting conventions.

8. **Pre-publication checks.** Reading-level, banned-phrase check, fact-pull surfacing against a knowledge base (where one exists), basic legal-risk flagging on a list of trigger phrases. Pattern work that humans drift on under deadline.

A worked Delegate List in the Chapter 2 template, for a B2B SaaS social manager:

| Task | Why pattern-shaped | Recommended AI tool category | Time saved | Quality risk if skipped |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Draft LinkedIn caption from approved core | Structure fixed; voice fits documented register | General LLM with brand prompt | 30 min/post | Low — caps voice on the margin |
| Generate 10–20 caption variants for A/B | Combinatorial variation | General LLM | 20 min/test | Low — testing surfaces the floor |
| Compress 1,500-word blog into Twitter/X thread | Format conversion is rule-following | General LLM | 45 min | Low-medium — hook needs human pass |
| Brainstorm 20 candidate angles for a campaign pillar | Bounded variation around a theme | General LLM | 30 min | Low — selection is human |
| Draft alt-text for image carousel (8 slides) | Description pattern; standards teachable | LLM with vision | 15 min | Low |
| Hashtag research for new campaign | Pattern recognition over trend data | LLM or specialty platform tool | 20 min | Medium — dated tags are common AI fail |
| Repost frame copy for industry-news repost | Frame conventions are documented | General LLM | 5 min/post | Low |
| Banned-phrase and reading-level pre-publication check | Rule-following | General LLM with brand list | 5 min/post | Medium — drift in a hurry |

These items are honest pattern work. The under-delegation failure mode of Chapter 1 lives here. If you are doing all eight by hand in 2026, you are losing five to seven hours a week to work AI can do at sufficient quality with your editing pass.

## The judgment-shaped work in this task (Guard List)

What in content creation currently requires a human:

1. **Voice calibration.** Whether a draft sounds like *this* brand. Voice is the residue of accumulated decisions about what the brand cares about, what it refuses to say, what its founder or its lead writer would never publish. AI imitates the surface — register, cadence, sentence-length distribution — and misses the depth. Ursula K. Le Guin's argument in *Steering the Craft* (1998) is that voice cannot be taught directly; it is practiced into being through accumulated decisions. The same argument applies to brand voice.

2. **Angle selection.** Which take, on this topic, does this brand actually believe? Which take advances the brand's position? Which take, given everything else the brand has said this quarter, is consistent? Angle selection is upstream of drafting. By the time you are editing the prose, the angle is already chosen — and the angle is where the voice lives.

3. **Cultural-moment response.** Whether to react at all to a moment in the broader culture. What tone the brand can credibly take given what it has said before. Which moments are the brand's to comment on and which are not. The failure mode is the brand jumping into a moment with AI-fluent copy that reads as opportunistic.

4. **Founder or named-executive voice.** The CEO's LinkedIn voice is the CEO's, not a stylesheet's. A post in the CEO's name that the CEO would never have written erodes the trust that made the CEO worth following. The same applies to named columnists, named partners, named anyone whose identity carries the post.

5. **Originality at the feed level.** Any single AI-drafted post can be original-enough. A feed of AI-drafted posts converges. Doshi and Hauser's finding generalizes: writers using the same models on the same prompts produce sets of outputs that lose distinctiveness. The Guard category here is the feed, not the post.

6. **Named-person references.** Any post that names a real individual — customer, partner, competitor, employee — carries reputational and sometimes legal risk that AI cannot weigh. Whether to publish, in what frame, with what consent.

7. **Sensitive-topic engagement.** Politics, health, identity, grief, controversy adjacent to the brand. The Guard activates when the topic is sensitive; the human decides whether to engage and, if so, on what terms.

8. **Controversial takes.** Posts that argue for something rather than describing it. The model can produce a confident argument on any side. Whether the brand can defend the argument afterward, and whether the argument is one the brand believes, is judgment.

9. **The decision to publish.** The most consequential Guard item. Whether *this* post should go up *now*. "AI drafted it" is not "AI can ship it."

Worked Guard List in the Chapter 2 template:

| Task | Why judgment-shaped | What goes wrong if delegated | Required human role | Escalation trigger |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Final voice pass on AI-drafted post | Voice is accumulated decisions; surface-imitable but depth-unique | Voice drifts to generic-SaaS register | Brand lead or experienced writer | Every AI-drafted post before publish |
| Angle selection for thought-leadership | Which take the brand actually believes | Post says something the brand will not defend | Brand lead + topic owner | Any thought-leadership post |
| CEO or named-executive byline | Voice belongs to the named individual | CEO publicly disowns the post; trust erodes | Executive or designated proxy | Any post under named byline |
| Cultural-moment reactive content | Tone judgment; opportunism risk | Brand reads as exploiting; community backlash | Brand lead | Any unplanned reactive content |
| Feed-level originality audit | Convergence detectable only across posts over time | Audience perceives flattening; engagement drops slowly | Brand lead, weekly | Weekly review cadence |
| Posts naming a customer, partner, or competitor | Reputational and consent risk | Trust damage; legal exposure | Brand lead + legal if material | Any post naming an individual |
| Sensitive-topic engagement | Topic carries collateral risk | Brand reads as tone-deaf; community pushback | Brand lead | Any topic on a maintained sensitive-list |
| Controversial argumentative post | Argument must be defensible | Brand reads as incoherent; positioning damage | Brand lead | Any argumentative angle |
| Publish/don't-publish on any AI-drafted post | Shipping is the consequential act | The post that should not have gone up does | Brand lead or designated publisher | Every AI-drafted post |

The escalation triggers are the operational layer. A Guard item without a trigger is a prohibition that gets ignored under deadline.

## A worked decomposition — one LinkedIn thought-leadership post

The framework's central move applied to one task. Imagine a B2B SaaS social manager publishing a 1,200-word LinkedIn essay in the CEO's voice on a topic the company wants to own.

1. *Topic selection.* What does the CEO want to be known for this quarter? **Guard.** Strategic; depends on positioning and the CEO's actual interests.

2. *Angle on the topic.* Which take does the CEO actually defend? **Guard.** Depends on knowing the CEO.

3. *Research synthesis.* Pull recent industry developments, supporting data, counterarguments. **Delegate.** Pattern-shaped retrieval and summary.

4. *Outline.* Structure the essay around the angle. **Delegate.** Pattern-shaped given the angle.

5. *First-draft prose.* Write the actual essay. **Delegate.** AI does this well; editing is fast.

6. *Voice pass.* Does this sound like the CEO? **Guard.** The hardest move. The pattern-shaped editor who knows the CEO's voice from years of writing for them is the +1 here.

7. *Opening hook.* The first 1–2 sentences. **Guard.** This is where voice is most exposed and where most engagement is decided. AI's instinct is generic-fluent; the CEO's instinct is specific.

8. *Closing line.* The take-home that the audience will quote. **Guard.** Same reasoning as the hook.

9. *Hashtags and tags.* Discoverability layer. **Delegate.** Pattern-shaped.

10. *Posting time and format tuning.* When and how. **Delegate.** Pattern-shaped.

11. *Pre-publication legal/brand check.* Any phrase that should not appear. **Delegate** (with human approval on flags). Pattern check; review on hits.

12. *Decision to publish.* The final call. **Guard.** The shipping decision.

13. *First-hour comment monitoring and prioritized response.* The relational layer. **Guard** (sorting is Delegate; responding is Guard).

Thirteen steps. Eight Delegate, five Guard. Without decomposition, the practitioner either delegates the whole essay (over-delegation; voice failure on 6, 7, 8) or hand-writes the whole thing (under-delegation; wasted time on 3, 4, 5, 9, 10, 11). The decomposition is what produces the right boundary.

A B2B SaaS practitioner who runs this decomposition for every CEO essay will spend roughly 90 minutes per essay where they previously spent 4–6 hours, and will produce essays that survive the CEO's read more reliably than the AI-only drafts they were producing six months ago.

## Do This with AI

A working playbook for content creation in 2026.

**Step 1 — Extract your voice in transferable terms (one-time, 60–90 min).** Sit down with 20–40 pieces of content your brand has published that you consider on-voice. Pull out: three brand values that recur; three communication taboos (things this brand never says or does); three signature phrases or framing devices; one piece of content that is decidedly *not* this brand, with a sentence on why. The artifact is a voice extraction worksheet. It becomes the input to every AI prompt for drafting.

**Step 2 — Build the drafting brief template.** Every AI drafting prompt should constrain on: audience (specific), format (specific), length (specific), angle (specific — drafted by you), tone (anchored to the voice worksheet), and one explicit "do not" derived from the taboos list. Generic prompts produce generic output; specific prompts produce drafts that can be edited fast.

**Step 3 — Draft, then voice-pass.** Use AI for the first draft. Then do an explicit voice pass before any other edit — read for whether this sounds like the brand. The voice pass is its own skill. Pre-AI, editors did this implicitly; post-AI, it has to be made explicit because the default AI output is fluent in a way that masks voice drift.

**Step 4 — Generate variation under human selection.** For testing, generate ten variants. Pick which one ships. Never let the model pick. The selection is where voice is asserted.

**Step 5 — Run the weekly feed-level audit.** Sample 5–10 pieces from the week, including AI-touched and not. Read them as the audience. Note whether the feed sounds like one brand or like several similar ones. Doshi and Hauser's finding shows up here, in production, in your work. Catch it weekly.

**Step 6 — Maintain the voice worksheet quarterly.** Voice evolves. The brand grew. The audience shifted. The taboos list moved. Update the worksheet and re-prompt.

**A sample drafting brief that works.**

> "Draft a 150-word LinkedIn caption for [audience: senior B2B SaaS marketers, 8+ years experience]. Format: caption + 3-line P.S. Angle: [your angle, one sentence]. Tone: anchored to this voice worksheet [paste]. Constraint: do not use the words 'unlock,' 'leverage,' 'game-changer,' or 'in today's fast-paced world.' Generate three variants with different opening hooks."

The brief constrains enough that the output is close to publishable on the first pass. Generic prompts produce drafts that need substantial editing; specific prompts produce drafts that need voice review only.

**A sample feed-audit prompt.**

> "Below are the last 10 LinkedIn captions this brand published. Three were AI-drafted with my voice pass; seven were human-drafted. Without me telling you which is which, identify any tonal patterns — words, phrases, sentence structures, opening moves — that appear in three or more captions and might constitute voice drift. Flag anything that reads as a default LLM phrasing rather than a brand-specific phrasing."

The audit is for your read, not the model's verdict. The model can flag candidates; you decide whether the candidates indicate drift.

## Never Do This with AI

Eight failure modes in content creation, each with the reason it fails.

1. **Never let AI choose which variant publishes.** Variant generation is pattern work; variant selection is voice work. Letting the model pick erodes voice by averaging across the generated set. Pick the variant yourself, or have a human editor pick.

2. **Never let AI write the opening hook of a thought-leadership post.** The hook is where voice is most exposed and where engagement is decided. AI's default is fluent-generic. Your hook should sound like you. Write the hook by hand even when you delegate the rest.

3. **Never publish AI-drafted content under a named individual's byline without that individual's read.** The Sports Illustrated lesson is the byline failure. A named byline is a contract with the reader that the named person stands behind the words. The named person has to read the words.

4. **Never use AI to fabricate identity.** No AI-generated bylines, no AI headshots, no AI-attributed quotes from people who do not exist or did not say them. This is a hard line. The contract collapses.

5. **Never delegate the angle.** The angle is the take, the position, the argument. The angle is where voice lives. AI can produce twenty candidate angles; you select among them. Letting AI select is letting the model decide what your brand believes.

6. **Never skip the weekly feed-level audit.** Doshi and Hauser's homogenization effect is structural to current models. The only way to catch it is to sample the feed over time. Per-post audits miss it because per-post the output is original-enough.

7. **Never react to a cultural moment without judgment review.** AI-fluent reactive content reads as opportunistic. The judgment is whether to react at all, on what angle, in what tone. The Guard activates on any unplanned reactive content.

8. **Never let AI write content involving a named customer, partner, or competitor without legal and brand review.** The model does not know the consent status, the relationship history, or the legal exposure. The cost of an avoidable mention is far higher than the cost of the review.

## The +1

For content creation, the +1 is voice. Specifically: voice as the accumulated record of the brand's decisions about what it cares about, what it refuses to say, and what the named humans on the team would actually publish under their names.

Le Guin's argument: voice cannot be taught directly. It is practiced into being. The same is true for brand voice. AI can imitate the markers — register, cadence, sentence-length distribution — and miss the depth. The +1 is the editor or writer who knows what the brand sounds like because they have been writing it.

Zora Neale Hurston walked back into Eatonville, Florida in 1928 with a notebook to record the speech of the community she had grown up in. She did not invent the dialogue in *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937); she recorded the community for years and then composed from the recording. A model trained on her finished novels could reproduce the surface — the cadences, the spellings, the call-and-response structure — and miss everything that mattered. Voice is community-embedded. It is recorded, not generated.

The same structural claim applies, modestly, to brand voice. Your brand has a voice because of years of decisions about what to say, what to refuse to say, what to ship, what to kill. The model has access to the decisions only as they appear in the published output. The +1 is the practitioner whose head holds the decisions that did not make it into the corpus — the post that was pulled, the angle that was rejected, the phrase the founder hates.

What the +1 brings:

- **The voice worksheet's actual reasoning.** The values, taboos, signature moves, anti-examples. AI uses the worksheet as a constraint; the +1 wrote the worksheet from a position only the brand's team can occupy.

- **The angle call.** Which take, on this topic, this brand defends. The model can produce twenty candidate angles; the +1 picks the one that fits.

- **The voice pass.** Reading for whether this sounds like the brand, after the model has produced fluent prose. The pass is its own skill, learned by reading and writing for the brand over time.

- **The hook and the close.** Where voice is most exposed and where the audience makes its read.

- **The publish/don't-publish call.** The shipping decision, in this moment, given everything the +1 knows about the brand and the audience and the moment.

- **The feed-level audit.** The weekly read of the brand's recent output as if you were the audience. Catching drift before it becomes flattening.

In the Heinz case, the +1 brought the judgment that "AI thinks ketchup looks like Heinz" was a campaign. In the Sports Illustrated case, the absence of a +1 — or the override of a +1 by someone willing to fabricate identity — was the failure. The technology was the same. The +1 was the difference between a campaign and a contract failure.

## Key terms

**Voice.** The accumulated record of the brand's decisions about what it cares about, what it refuses to say, and what the named humans on the team would publish under their names. Surface-imitable, depth-unique. Guard category. Le Guin's craft argument applies; Hurston's community-embedded argument applies.

**Style.** The surface markers of voice — register, cadence, sentence-length distribution, vocabulary choices. Style can be partly captured in a stylesheet. Voice cannot. Style is Delegate-adjacent (it can be prompted for); voice is Guard.

**Angle.** The take, position, or argument the post advances. Upstream of drafting. Guard category. Where voice lives. Selecting the angle is the judgment move that determines what the brand says, not just how the brand says it.

**Originality at the feed level.** The property of a brand's output across time that lets an audience identify the brand from the writing. Distinct from novelty at the post level. Per-post originality is overrated; feed-level originality is load-bearing. Doshi and Hauser's homogenization finding makes feed-level originality a Guard category.

**Voice worksheet.** A one-page artifact extracting brand values, taboos, signature phrases, and anti-examples from existing on-voice content. The input to every AI drafting prompt. Maintained quarterly.

**Drafting brief.** The specific prompt structure that constrains AI output on audience, format, length, angle, tone, and at least one explicit "do not." Specific briefs produce drafts that need only voice review; generic briefs produce drafts that need substantial rewriting.

**Voice pass.** The first edit on an AI-drafted piece, reading specifically for whether it sounds like the brand. Pre-AI, editors did this implicitly. Post-AI, it must be made explicit because fluent AI output masks voice drift.

**Feed-level audit.** A weekly sample of the brand's recent content read as the audience would read it, looking for convergence patterns — phrases, structures, openings — that suggest the feed is drifting toward generic-LLM register. The Guard practice that catches Doshi-Hauser homogenization in production.

**Homogenization (Doshi-Hauser).** The empirical finding from *Science Advances* (2024) that AI-assisted writers individually produce more creative outputs on average, while the collective set of outputs converges. Fewer distinct ideas across writers using the same models. Structural to current generation; the spine of the Chapter 3 Guard List.
