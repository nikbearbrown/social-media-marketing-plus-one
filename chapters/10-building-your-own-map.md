# Chapter 10 — Building Your Own Map

*Domain calibration: turning the framework into your Delegate/Guard List*

---

A mid-size registered investment adviser in the Midwest — roughly $1.4 billion in assets under management — rolled out an AI content acceleration program in Q2 2024. The plan was unobjectionable on paper: a marketing coordinator would draft LinkedIn posts in ChatGPT, the chief compliance officer would approve in batch on Fridays, and the firm would triple its publishing cadence inside a quarter.

Three things happened in the first month. Output tripled, as predicted. The CCO's Friday review went from 45 minutes to four hours, because every post now needed line-by-line testing against the SEC Marketing Rule's prohibitions on testimonials, performance implications, and language that could be read as a guarantee. The approval bottleneck had not been eliminated — it had moved upstream and grown. Then in week six, one post slipped through. A coordinator had asked the model to "make it punchier," and the model produced a sentence that read, in context, as a performance promise. It was live for 90 minutes before a junior compliance analyst caught it. The firm's outside counsel spent two weeks documenting the incident in case of a subsequent examination.

The lesson the firm drew was not "stop using AI." It was that the Delegate/Guard map they had borrowed from a general-purpose marketing podcast had no idea what a registered investment adviser's content even is. Static, pre-approved, retainable content under FINRA rules lives in one regulatory universe. Interactive real-time response lives in another. Performance language lives in a third. A delegation map calibrated for a DTC apparel brand or a B2B SaaS startup will get an RIA fined.

The map has to be yours.

---

That sentence — the map has to be yours — is the entire argument of this chapter, and it is worth defending carefully rather than just asserting, because the temptation to borrow is real and the borrowed maps look plausible from the outside.

Every previous chapter in this book has been making a general claim about a category of work: community management, analytics, influencer partnerships, crisis response. The claim in each case was the same in structure — here is what AI currently handles well, here is what currently requires a human, here is where the boundary sits. That structure was useful because it named the principle. But the principle is not the map. The map is what you produce when you apply the principle to your specific work, your specific org, your specific regulatory exposure, and your specific audience.

The RIA case illustrates what happens when you skip that application step. The borrowed map was not wrong about marketing in general. It was wrong about this firm's situation specifically, because the firm's situation involved regulatory constraints the general map could not have known about and did not ask about. A framework calibrated on social media marketing for consumer brands treats FTC endorsement rules as the relevant ceiling. For a FINRA-regulated firm, FTC rules are the floor, and the ceiling is considerably lower.

But regulatory exposure is only one axis of calibration. The other two are shape and tail-risk, and these vary just as much across contexts, though less visibly.

Shape is the distinction the whole book has been building: pattern-shaped work follows a transferable recipe; judgment-shaped work depends on context the recipe cannot capture. You know this distinction by now. What you may not have mapped is which tasks in your specific workflow fall where. A DTC founder's "is today the day to post about my father's funeral" is judgment-shaped in a way that no classification framework produces from the outside — only she knows the relationship between her personal narrative and her audience's tolerance for it. A hospital system's "does this patient story require explicit HIPAA authorization" is judgment-shaped in a way that requires knowing whether the poster is identifiable by the 18 identifiers under 45 CFR Part 164, which requires a person who has read the rule and is accountable to the institution.

Tail-risk is subtler and the most commonly omitted axis. The question is not whether a bad post is embarrassing — almost every bad post is embarrassing. The question is whether a bad post becomes a news cycle, a regulatory inquiry, or a customer-trust event from which the brand does not recover at normal speed. The same content mistake that costs a consumer apparel brand a week of goodwill costs a financial advisory firm two weeks of outside counsel and a shadow over the next regulatory examination. Tail-risk is a function of audience expectations, regulatory scrutiny, and the gap between how the brand presents itself and what it could actually be shown to have done. Only you can assess that gap for your specific situation.

---

There is a complication in building the map that is worth naming directly, because it is the reason most practitioners struggle with the Guard List more than the Delegate List.

The Delegate List is built by examining your workflow and asking: which of these tasks could a model do at comparable quality with less human time? The question is empirical. You run the task, you compare the output, you decide. The answer is usually clearer than you expected — most of the pattern-shaped volume is genuinely delegable once you stop doing it by hand and look at the result.

The Guard List is built by a different question, and it is harder: which of these tasks am I doing myself, and why? The difficulty is that the "why" is often not articulable. David Autor, drawing on Michael Polanyi's 1966 work on tacit knowledge, named this Polanyi's Paradox: we know more than we can tell. The experienced practitioner who guards a certain kind of post — who insists on writing it personally, who flinches at the idea of an AI draft — often cannot explain the reasoning in terms that would satisfy a skeptic. The reasoning is embedded in their judgment, not stored as an explicit rule.

This is not a weakness to be embarrassed about. It is what tacit knowledge is. But it creates a specific problem for map-building: the Guard List you produce by introspection is likely to be incomplete, because some of your judgment-shaped work is invisible to you until you watch yourself flinch.

The productive method is to run the audit in two passes. First pass: classify your task inventory along the three axes as best you can. Second pass: look at every task you put on the Guard List and ask whether you can explain why in a sentence a skeptical compliance officer would find convincing. The tasks where you cannot produce that sentence are the ones where Polanyi's Paradox is operating. They may still belong on the Guard List — the flinch is often tracking something real — but they require the additional work of articulation before the map is defensible to anyone besides yourself.

---

The practical audit runs in roughly half a working day, and the structure matters more than the speed.

You begin with inventory. Not a theoretical list of tasks in your role — a list of the specific tasks you actually touched in the last two working weeks. The distinction is important because theoretical lists include the tasks you are supposed to be doing and omit the tasks that actually consume your time, whereas the actual-weeks list inverts this. Use the chapter sequence from this book as a checklist — content creation, community management, analytics, paid social, influencer, crisis monitoring — and add brand-specific tasks the framework does not cover. Thirty to forty line items is the target.

Then classify each item on the three axes: shape, regulatory weight, and reputational tail-risk. The classification is a draft, not a verdict. Tasks that are judgment-shaped, heavily regulated, and high tail-risk go on the Guard List by default. Tasks that are pattern-shaped, lightly regulated, and low tail-risk go on the Delegate List by default. Everything else is a boundary case, and boundary cases are where the map does its most important work — because they are the tasks most likely to be classified by default rather than by decision, and default classification is where the borrowed map gets firms into trouble.

After classification comes the tool audit, which is the step most practitioners skip and which makes the map describe a workflow that does not exist. The Salesforce and Microsoft workforce surveys from 2024–2025 both documented that the majority of knowledge workers using AI at work do so on personal accounts with unsanctioned tools. This is not a moral failure — it is what happens when organizational AI policy lags individual capability. But a delegation map that does not account for shadow AI use cannot be calibrated against actual risk. Surface the actual tool use without punishment, because you cannot calibrate what you cannot see.

---

Three contrast cases show why the same audit method produces different maps.

A founder-led DTC apparel brand at roughly $30 million in revenue has built its audience on the founder's voice — specific, personal, sometimes confessional. The Delegate List is wide on production work: scheduling, paid copy variation, comment triage on low-stakes mentions, analytics narration. The Guard List is narrow but load-bearing: anything attributed to the founder's voice, anything responding to a customer service complaint, anything political, anything about supply chain that could become contractual. The critical Guard item is not a regulatory rule. It is the founder's judgment about which posts are hers to make — and when. A model cannot determine whether today is the day to share something personal. She has to.

A Midwest academic medical center at roughly $3 billion in revenue operates under HIPAA, which makes the 18 patient identifiers under 45 CFR Part 164 the third rail of everything. The Delegate List is real but constrained: disease-awareness content with no patient connection, generic wellness content, event promotion, clinical staff recruiting. The Guard List is defined almost entirely by PHI exposure: anything that could be a patient testimonial without HIPAA authorization, any response to a public patient post, anything touching specific clinical outcomes. The practical consequence is that the human reviewer at this institution is not checking brand voice — they are checking identifiability, which is a legal determination that requires training, not taste.

A Series B SaaS startup at roughly $40 million ARR has a light regulatory regime. The constraint is sales-marketing alignment. The Guard List is organized around the CRM: anything touching a named account in an active deal cycle, any executive quote attributed to the founder without sign-off, any response to a customer in a public escalation, any competitive comparison. The gating Guard item is information the model cannot see — what is happening in open deals right now — which means the boundary between Delegate and Guard is partly a function of CRM integration, not just content type.

The same audit method. Three different maps. The method generalizes; the lists do not.

---

The map is a living document, not a one-time artifact, and treating it as static is one of the six ways practitioners reliably break it. The capability of the tools changes quarterly. Regulatory guidance changes — the FTC's endorsement rules were updated in 2023; the SEC Marketing Rule generated examination guidance in 2024; the EU AI Act's transparency obligations under Articles 50 and 52 are phased and still being interpreted. Your org changes. Your audience changes. A map written in January that has not been touched by October is describing a situation that no longer exists.

The other five failure modes are worth naming briefly, because each is common and each has a specific remedy.

Borrowing someone else's map wholesale is the Meridian failure — the borrowed map has not done your regulatory analysis. Skipping the tool audit means the map describes a workflow that does not exist. Letting AI write the Guard List unsupervised produces a plausible-looking list that is precisely least reliable where reliability matters most, because regulatory specificity is exactly where the model's training data is noisiest. Outsourcing the judgment-surface articulation — asking the model to explain why you personally guard a certain kind of post — produces a confident-sounding answer that is probably not your actual reason; the discomfort of articulating tacit knowledge is the work. And building the map without compliance produces shelfware: a map that compliance has not seen is theatre, and you will revert to manual drafting the first time something feels uncertain because nobody has authorized you to delegate.

In every previous chapter, the +1 was the human review of a specific AI output — the person who reads the draft before it posts, who makes the crisis call, who rewrites the first sentence of the outreach email. In this chapter the +1 steps up a level. You are no longer reviewing a single output. You are designing the system that determines which outputs get reviewed and by whom.

That is a different kind of accountability. When a post goes wrong because the map allowed it, you are answerable for the map, not just for the post. The answerability is what makes the map worth anything. A map that nobody is accountable for is a list of suggestions that will be ignored under pressure, which is exactly when the map matters most.

The map is also the artifact in which your local knowledge becomes operational. The model does not know your regulatory exposure. It does not know your audience's specific tolerances. It does not know what happened inside your org last quarter. It does not know what your CCO will and will not sign off on. All of that knowledge lives in you, and the map is how you convert it into a system rather than leaving it as an informal habit that does not survive your departure or the next person hired into your role.

Mary Parker Follett, writing in the 1920s on what she called the law of the situation, argued that authority in an organization should derive from the specific facts of the case rather than from a generic title or rule. The delegation map is the law of your situation — a document that derives its authority from the specific facts of your org, your industry, your tools, and your audience. AI can offer a generic rule. Only you can describe the situation.

That is the chapter 10 +1: the practitioner as calibrator. Pattern is universal. Judgment is local. The map is local because judgment is local. Build it yourself.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Generate and examine**

Run the Chapter 10 inventory-and-classification prompt on your actual two-week task list. Review the model's proposed classification on all three axes — shape, regulatory weight, and tail-risk. Find the five tasks where your own classification differs most from the model's. For each disagreement: is the model's error in the shape assessment, the regulatory read, or the tail-risk estimate? What does the pattern of errors tell you about where your specific situation falls outside the general framework?

**Exercise 2 — Apply to known context**

Take one of the three contrast cases in the chapter — the DTC founder, the hospital system, or the SaaS startup — and run a full map-building audit as if it were your org. Produce a draft Delegate List and Guard List. Then compare your output to what the chapter describes. Where did you put tasks the chapter would guard? Where did you delegate what the chapter would protect? What assumptions drove the differences?

**Exercise 3 — Stress-test a specific claim**

The chapter claims that the Guard List is the part of the map most likely to be wrong when produced by AI without human calibration. Test this: ask a language model to produce a Guard List for a regulated industry you know — financial advisory, healthcare, legal services, or another sector with documented regulatory constraints. Then audit the model's output against the actual regulatory text. How many items on the model's Guard List are wrong, missing, or too vague to be actionable? What does the error rate imply about the minimum human-review requirement for the Guard List specifically?

**Exercise 4 — Draft or audit a professional deliverable**

Using the Chapter 10 protocol, produce a socialization memo for your actual or hypothetical map. The memo should frame the Delegate/Guard map as a risk-management document, specify who has reviewed the regulatory assumptions, name the boundary cases and the re-audit schedule, and identify who in the org has authority to approve changes to the Guard List. Draft a first version using a language model, then audit it against your actual organizational structure and your CCO's or manager's known concerns. Rewrite every section where the model's framing would not survive the first conversation with compliance.
