# Chapter 9 — Platform-Specific Execution

*Format optimization vs. community norms, platform culture*

---

## Opening case

A B2B cybersecurity vendor — call it Lattice Defense, Series C, primarily enterprise sales — ran a thought-leadership push in Q3 2025 around a published security research finding. (Illustrative composite; the cross-posting failure pattern is documented across multiple agency case studies and *Marketing Brew* coverage 2024–2025.) The CMO commissioned one strong piece of source content: a 12-minute panel discussion with the firm's lead researcher and a named industry analyst. The agency reformatted the same content across four platforms in 48 hours using a now-standard AI repurposing workflow: a 9:16 vertical cut for TikTok and Reels, a 16:9 thumbnail-and-trim for YouTube, a four-card carousel for LinkedIn, a quoted-clip thread for X. Captions were AI-generated per platform with appropriate length and hashtag conventions.

Distribution looked, at first, fine. The LinkedIn carousel performed against the firm's account benchmark. The YouTube clip pulled modest organic traffic. The TikTok and Reels cuts pulled essentially nothing — under 400 views combined across 30 days against an account with 11K followers. The X thread pulled engagement but the wrong kind: replies from accounts hostile to the firm's category positioning, with the original content not being shared at all.

The diagnosis was not technical. The TikTok cut was a 9:16 vertical reformat of a content piece whose pacing, opening, and visual vocabulary were built for a long-form panel format. The pacing was wrong for the platform; the opening (a host introduction) was wrong for the first three seconds where TikTok's recommendation system decides whether to push the video into non-follower feeds. The Reels cut had the same problem. The X thread used a tone calibrated for LinkedIn — analytical, third-person, careful — that on X reads as press-release-pasted-in. The captions, AI-generated per platform, had the recognizable AI-Caption signature that audiences and (anecdotally) the algorithms in 2024–2025 penalize. [verify on the algorithm-penalty claim — observable in reach data, contested as causal]

The CMO concluded — wrongly, in the way the chapter is trying to push back against — that "TikTok doesn't work for B2B." It can. Documented enterprise-software accounts on TikTok in 2024–2025 (the LinkedIn-native vendors that have extended to TikTok with re-voiced content, the developer-tooling brands building creator collaborations) demonstrate this. What did not work was treating cross-platform execution as a format-adaptation problem when it is a *voice and culture* adaptation problem with a format-adaptation layer underneath.

The chapter's aphorism: *AI can give you the format. Only the human can give you the fit.*

A note before the lists. This chapter has the highest aging risk in the book. Platform mechanics, algorithm behaviors, and creator-monetization rules change on the order of months. The principles below — distribution model, first-class format, community norms, re-voicing required — are durable. The specific platform observations are snapshots as of late 2025–early 2026. Currently requires re-verification at draft time and again at workshop delivery; the framing throughout this chapter assumes both.

## The pattern-shaped work in this task (Delegate List)

The following pieces are pattern-shaped and AI handles them well today:

1. **Aspect-ratio adaptation across platforms.** Re-cropping 16:9 source to 9:16, 1:1, 4:5. Mechanical. Reliable. Saves real hours.

2. **Length cutting.** A five-minute video to a 60-second cut to a 15-second hook. AI tools identify candidate cut points, generate transitions, produce variant lengths. Human selects which to publish.

3. **Caption length and format adaptation per platform.** TikTok 100–150 character captions vs. LinkedIn 1,200–2,200 character long-form vs. X 250-character post vs. Instagram 125 characters above the fold. The mechanical adaptation is pattern; the voice is not.

4. **Hashtag and keyword research per platform.** Each platform has different hashtag culture (TikTok favors broad-to-narrow ladder; Instagram has reduced hashtag weight since 2023 algorithm changes; LinkedIn uses sparingly; X minimally). Tool-supported.

5. **Thumbnail and cover variant generation.** YouTube thumbnails, TikTok cover frames, LinkedIn document carousel covers. A/B variant generation is straightforward; the selection is human.

6. **Hook A/B variant generation.** Multiple opening-three-second variants for testing. Especially valuable on recommendation-first platforms where the opening determines distribution.

7. **Per-platform optimal-time scheduling.** Engagement-pattern data per account, per platform, surfaces optimal windows. Tools deliver this competently.

8. **Alt-text and accessibility-text generation.** A real win for accessibility-as-execution; the AI-generated first draft is competent and a human review for context-specific accuracy closes the gap.

9. **Cross-platform performance reporting.** Pulling engagement, reach, completion-rate, share-rate figures per platform into a single dashboard. Pattern.

10. **Translation and localization across platforms.** First-pass translation is fast; idiomatic and cultural adaptation — particularly across the regional TikToks (TikTok-US vs. TikTok-Brazil vs. TikTok-India have meaningfully different content cultures) — currently requires human review.

The Delegate List is wide. None of it touches the voice or the fit. All of it is mechanical adaptation that buys you hours to do the judgment work.

## The judgment-shaped work in this task (Guard List)

Platform-specific execution currently requires a human for the following:

1. **Platform-mix strategy.** Which platforms the brand commits to and which it does not. The default — be everywhere — is usually wrong. Concentration on two or three platforms where the brand's audience lives and the team can produce genuinely native content outperforms thin coverage. AI provides demographic data; the strategic call is yours.

2. **Format choice for a given message.** The judgment that this message belongs as a TikTok and not as a LinkedIn post, or vice versa, or as both with different angles. AI can adapt format; AI cannot decide that the source idea is wrong for one platform and right for another.

3. **Voice and tone per platform.** The continuously-updated reading of what the platform's culture currently rewards. The "Duolingo voice on TikTok" (documented through Duolingo's published case studies, the social-team interviews in *Adweek* and *Modern Retail*, and the publicly disclosed app-install metrics in Duolingo's earnings filings) and the "Ryanair voice on TikTok" (deliberately low-production-value, in-on-the-joke, 2022–present) are not templates AI can apply. They are continuously refreshed reads of platform culture by specific humans.

4. **Trend participation.** Whether to ride a current TikTok sound, an Instagram audio meme, an X reply-format, a LinkedIn engagement pattern. The judgment: is the trend still rising or has it peaked; does it fit the brand; can the team produce in the trend's idiom credibly; will participation read as opportunistic. AI flags trends; the judgment is human.

5. **Community-norm reading.** The implicit rules that produce silent distribution penalties — reduced reach, suppressed comments, reduced share rate — without being announced as penalties. Different on Reddit (hostile-to-corporate culture, sub-by-sub variance) than on LinkedIn (rewards long personal-story posts) than on TikTok (rewards completion rate above almost everything else). The norms are nowhere documented; reading them is human work.

6. **Hook selection.** Which of the AI-generated opening variants gets published. On recommendation-first platforms the first one to three seconds determines whether the post is distributed at all. The selection is brand-voice judgment.

7. **Comment-engagement policy.** Whether and how the brand replies on each platform. LinkedIn replies extend reach (treated as content). X replies are expected and read as in-character or out-of-character. TikTok replies can be turned into video responses (a significant format opportunity). Instagram replies are mostly conversational. The reply policy is per-platform and is a brand-voice call.

8. **Refusal.** The judgment that the brand should not be on a particular platform at all, regardless of audience size. The B2B vendor that should not be on Instagram. The serious-news brand that should not be on certain X formats. The regulated-industry brand whose disclosure obligations make TikTok-native execution operationally impossible. Refusal is the hardest call in this chapter because the default is presence.

9. **Platform-political reading.** When platform-level changes — ownership change, policy change, controversy, regulatory action — require re-evaluating presence. The X trajectory under post-2022 ownership and policy changes [verify currency]; the TikTok U.S. divestment / ban dynamics under the Protecting Americans from Foreign Adversary Controlled Applications Act (signed April 2024) and subsequent litigation [verify outcome — likely shifted between drafting and reading]; LinkedIn's video-first pivot. Each of these is a judgment call about presence the model cannot make for you.

## Per-platform brief

A page each for the four platforms in the table above. Snapshots. Verify at draft time.

**TikTok.** Distribution model: recommendation-first; the For You feed pushes content into the feeds of users who do not follow the account, weighted heavily on completion rate, rewatch, and shares. (Kabir & Madden 2023 at FAccT — sock-puppet audit study — found the system converges on interest signals within roughly 40 minutes of watch behavior; treat the figure as one rigorous data point.) [verify against more recent academic audit work, e.g., Wang et al. 2024 NYU CSMP working paper] First-class format: short vertical video. Community norms: native production over polish, opening hook in the first 1–3 seconds, sound is part of the content (muting reduces completion rate substantially), trend participation rewarded but timing-sensitive. What AI is good at here: cropping, length variants, hook A/B generation, caption length adaptation. What AI is bad at here: platform-native voice (the AI-TikTok-caption signature reads as such), trend-timing judgment, knowing whether a sound is rising / peaking / stale.

**Instagram.** Distribution model: mixed; Reels weighted toward recommendation (Mosseri's 2022–2024 explanatory posts make this explicit), Feed and Stories mostly follower-first, Explore recommendation-first. First-class format: Reels, then carousel; static single-image posts have lost relative algorithmic weight since the 2021–2022 Reels pivot. Community norms: aesthetic polish higher than TikTok; carousel as a "swipe-through educational" format has stable returns. Mosseri's specifically-cited "shares per reach" metric matters more than likes for Reels distribution. [verify against most recent Mosseri post] What AI is good at: carousel-layout generation, caption variants, Reels editing. What AI is bad at: the aesthetic judgment that makes a feed read as cohesive, the voice work that distinguishes a brand-Instagram from a content-mill-Instagram.

**LinkedIn.** Distribution model: follower-first with knowledge-graph extension; LinkedIn's August 2023 "knowledge and advice" emphasis pivot (documented in the LinkedIn Engineering Blog series 2022–2024) made comment depth and dwell time more load-bearing for distribution than they had been. First-class format: long-form text post (1,000–2,000 characters) and document carousel; native video has grown but is second-class. Community norms: long personal-story posts perform; named individual contributors out-perform brand accounts; professional voice but warmer than corporate-website voice. Engagement extends reach (comments treated as further content). LinkedIn Thought Leader Ads launched 2023 [verify currency] are a real format with real distribution. What AI is good at: long-form first drafts, document-carousel layout, caption-of-video structure. What AI is bad at: the personal-story voice that lands as authentic from a named individual rather than as ghostwritten.

**X (formerly Twitter).** Distribution model: follower-first with limited recommendation; the For You feed exists but the open-source release (March 2023, github.com/twitter/the-algorithm) [verify — the release has not been substantively updated since 2023; treat as a snapshot] documents the ranking factors at the time and predates significant subsequent changes. The 2023 API pricing changes substantially restructured the third-party tool ecosystem; this chapter does not bet on X's trajectory and the chapter's reader should verify current platform state at use time. First-class format: short text; images and video second-class. Community norms: in-the-moment, opinionated, reply-driven; the Wendy's account (the 2017 #NuggsForCarter era is the canonical case) established the reply-as-content pattern that has aged interestingly as the platform itself has changed. What AI is good at: quoted-clip extraction, thread-structure first drafts. What AI is bad at: the cultural-moment read for whether to engage at all on a specific topic, the reply judgment, the assessment of which conversations to enter.

A short note on each of the platforms this chapter does not give a full brief to: YouTube (Shorts and long-form behave like different platforms; treat them separately; long-form rewards depth, Shorts is functionally a recommendation-first competitor to TikTok). Reddit (hostile-to-corporate culture, sub-by-sub variance, the 2023 API changes pushed the brand-presence model toward in-sub-participation rather than brand-account broadcasting). Pinterest (vertical-image-first, evergreen distribution windows much longer than other platforms — content can pull traffic for years). The international platforms named above. The chapter's reader who works on these should read the durable principles here and consult current platform-native sources at execution time.

## Do This with AI

A working protocol for one campaign across platforms.

**Step 1 — Platform-mix decision (human, 30 min).** Before any production, decide which platforms this campaign appears on. Not all of them. Use brand audience data and current platform demographics (Pew Research Center's "Social Media Use" annual reports for U.S.; Reuters Institute Digital News Report for international; Ofcom Online Nation for UK) [verify most recent year]. Document why each chosen platform is in and each excluded platform is out.

**Step 2 — Source-content production (human-led, AI-supported).** Build one core piece of content optimized for the platform you will lean on hardest. Not a piece optimized for nothing. The Lattice case was a piece optimized for a panel format that was then chopped up; the better approach is a piece optimized for one primary platform with secondary cuts as deliberate adaptations.

**Step 3 — Per-platform adaptation (AI, 30–60 min per platform).** Run the format-adaptation workflow per platform: aspect ratio, length, caption variants, hashtag selection, hook variants. AI handles the mechanics.

**Step 4 — Per-platform re-voicing (human, 30–60 min per platform).** This is the load-bearing step. Rewrite the caption in the platform's voice. Re-cut the opening if it is wrong for the platform's hook expectations. Replace the AI-generated thumbnail with one chosen against the platform's aesthetic. Decide the comment-engagement policy for this post on this platform.

**Step 5 — Trend / cultural-moment check (human, 15 min per platform).** Is the timing right for this post on this platform today? Is there a current trend the brand should ride or avoid? Is there a competing news cycle that will swallow distribution? AI can surface signals; the call is yours.

**Step 6 — Hook selection (human, 5 min per platform).** Pick the opening that goes out. The 3-second decision on recommendation-first platforms.

**Step 7 — Schedule (AI, 5 min).** Per-platform optimal time. The tools do this well.

**Step 8 — Post-publication monitoring (AI watching, human reading, ongoing).** Track engagement against platform-specific benchmarks. Read the comments directly on each platform. Note where the platform-native version outperformed and where it underperformed.

**Worked output — Lattice Defense, take two.** Source: same 12-minute panel. Platform-mix decision: lean hardest on LinkedIn (B2B audience, long-form format aligned with content), with secondary distribution on YouTube long-form (audience that exists for the deeper version) and X (engagement with the research community). TikTok and Reels are deliberately excluded — the format mismatch is too severe for a re-voicing budget the team can credibly fund this quarter, and audience presence is thin enough that the opportunity cost is low. The decision to exclude is documented.

For LinkedIn: long-form text post written by the researcher in first person, with a four-card carousel of the key findings, with the video clip as the third element. The researcher's own voice — written, not ghostwritten — is the load-bearing element. AI-supported draft; human edit for voice; researcher review and sign-off.

For YouTube long-form: full 12-minute panel published with a researcher-led 90-second intro re-recorded for the platform.

For X: a thread by the researcher's personal account (not the brand account) with quoted clips, in a tone calibrated to the research community.

Distribution outcome (illustrative): LinkedIn at 6–8x the original cross-post version; YouTube modestly improved; X engagement positive and from accounts adjacent to the firm's actual buyer graph. Same source content. Materially different campaign.

## Never Do This with AI

Seven failure modes specific to AI-in-platform-execution.

1. **Never cross-post without re-voicing.** The audience and the algorithm read format and pacing signals that detect cross-posting. The same content across platforms without re-cutting and re-voicing materially under-performs the platform-native version. (Multiple agency case studies in *Modern Retail* and *Marketing Brew* 2024–2025 document the engagement deltas.) [verify against current case literature]

2. **Never publish AI-generated captions unedited.** The AI-Caption signature is recognizable to audiences and (anecdotally, observable in reach data) penalized by the algorithms. Human edit for voice is the difference between a post that reads as native and one that reads as content-mill output.

3. **Never delegate the platform-mix decision.** "Be everywhere" is the default and it is wrong for most brands. The decision to commit to two or three platforms credibly, and to refuse the others, is brand strategy. AI provides data; you decide.

4. **Never trust the AI's trend-participation recommendation.** Tools flag trends. Trends are rising / peaking / stale, and the judgment of which a current trend is — and of whether the brand should ride it credibly — is human work. Late participation in a stale trend reads as opportunistic and ages badly.

5. **Never let AI run reply engagement unedited.** The reply policy per platform is brand-voice judgment. An AI-auto-replied brand account on X or TikTok produces consistently flat replies that audiences read as flat. Worse, AI reply generation under live cultural conditions (a major news event, a sensitive moment in the category) is exactly when the cost of an off-tone reply is highest.

6. **Never assume the platform's own AI tools are safe defaults.** All major platforms have or are testing AI-generated content features — TikTok's AI avatar tools, Meta's AI-character interactions, LinkedIn's AI post drafting, YouTube's AI tools [verify currency]. Platform-native AI features sometimes produce content that gets labeled or de-prioritized by the same platform's recommendation system; the rules are evolving. The EU AI Act (Articles 50 and 52, phased entry through 2026) [verify] and DSA recommender-system transparency requirements (Articles 27, 38, 39, 40) are reshaping what platforms must disclose about AI-content labeling. Read the labeling consequences before using platform AI features in production content.

7. **Never declare a platform "doesn't work for our category" after a cross-post failure.** The Lattice CMO's wrong conclusion. Document the failure mode (mismatch, voice, format, timing) before generalizing to the platform.

## The +1

What you bring that AI cannot, in this chapter specifically:

- **The platform-mix call.** Two or three platforms the brand commits to credibly outperforms thin coverage across seven. The call requires reading audience presence, team capacity, regulatory constraints, brand-voice fit. AI surfaces inputs; you decide.

- **The voice read per platform.** The Duolingo voice on TikTok, the Ryanair voice on TikTok, the long-personal-story voice on LinkedIn — these are not templates. They are continuously-refreshed readings of platform culture by specific humans who watch the platform daily. Mary Katherine Goddard, running the *Maryland Journal* and serving as Baltimore's postmaster from 1775, made exactly this kind of execution call when she printed the first Declaration of Independence broadside to include the signers' names (January 1777, the "Goddard Broadside") — a content-format decision that read the medium (broadside), the audience (a colonial public demanding accountability), and the assertion-of-legitimacy that required names, simultaneously. Platform-native execution is older than social media; it is what you do when you read medium, audience, and norms together.

- **The refusal call.** The judgment that the brand should not be on a platform at all. The hardest decision in this chapter because the default is presence. Robert L. Vann, editor and publisher of the *Pittsburgh Courier* from 1910 to 1940, built the most widely circulated Black newspaper in the U.S. (peak circulation around 200,000 in the early 1940s) by committing to a specific audience's frame — including the famous Double V campaign during WWII — and refusing the dominant publications' frame. Platform-native execution sometimes requires committing to the audience's frame; sometimes it requires refusing the platform entirely. Both calls are yours.

- **The trend-timing read.** Rising, peaking, stale. AI sees the volume curve; you see the cultural temperature.

- **The hook selection.** The 1–3 second decision that determines distribution on recommendation-first platforms. AI generates variants; you pick.

- **The reply judgment.** Whether to engage on this post, with this person, on this platform, today.

- **The platform-political read.** Ownership changes, policy changes, regulatory actions — when these require re-evaluating presence.

The +1 in this chapter is the practitioner who lets AI handle format and runs voice and culture themselves. The format work is real and worth delegating. The fit work is the campaign.

## Key terms

**First-class format.** The content format the platform's recommendation system rewards disproportionately. TikTok: short vertical video. Instagram: Reels. YouTube: long-form video (Shorts second-class). LinkedIn: text and document carousels. X: short text. Producing in a second-class format is allowed but yields lower distribution.

**Distribution model.** The platform's bias between recommendation-first (content pushed into feeds of users who do not follow the account; small accounts can reach large audiences) and follower-first (reach bounded by audience size). Determines campaign mechanics fundamentally.

**Recommendation-first vs. follower-first.** The two ends of the distribution-model spectrum. TikTok and YouTube Shorts sit at the recommendation-first end; LinkedIn and X (currently) sit closer to follower-first; Instagram and Facebook sit in between and have been moving toward recommendation-first.

**Community norms.** The implicit rules of voice, format, posting cadence, and acceptable promotion that are not in the platform's documentation. Violating community norms produces distribution penalties that are not announced as penalties. Reading the norms is human work.

**Platform-native.** Content that respects the platform's first-class format, voice conventions, and community norms; reads to the audience as belonging on the platform rather than being imported from another.

**Cross-posting.** The practice of publishing the same content across platforms without re-cutting or re-voicing. Empirically under-performs the platform-native version. The efficiency is illusory.

**Completion rate.** The percentage of viewers who watch the video to the end. Heavily weighted in TikTok's recommendation system; weighted in Reels; less in long-form YouTube where watch-time-minutes is the equivalent signal.

**Shares per reach.** Mosseri's explicitly-named Reels metric that matters more than likes for distribution. The platform-specific signal that the team should optimize against rather than the legacy default.

**Dwell time.** The time a user spends with a post in-feed. LinkedIn-load-bearing. AI-assisted optimization works for length and structure; the voice that produces actual dwell is human.

**Hook.** The first one to three seconds (video) or first sentence (text) that determines whether the post is distributed further or skipped. On recommendation-first platforms, the hook is the post's distribution.

**Trend half-life.** The window during which participation in a current trend reads as timely rather than late. Compressed substantially over the past five years; currently measured in days to small numbers of weeks for most TikTok and Instagram trends.

**Silent distribution penalty.** Reduced reach, suppressed comments, or reduced share rate applied by a platform without being announced as a penalty. Often the consequence of community-norm violation. Currently observable in reach data; not currently documented in platform-published policy.
